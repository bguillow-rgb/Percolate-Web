The cupping spoon is one of the most unglamorous tools in specialty coffee. A simple piece of bent metal that almost every serious taster owns and almost nobody stops to explain. If you've watched a roaster crack a crust, skim two spoons across the surface, then loudly slurp a mouthful of coffee, you've seen the spoon in action. Here's what it actually is, why it's shaped the way it is, and how to use one properly at home.

## What exactly is a cupping spoon and why does it look the way it does?

A cupping spoon is designed for the tasting procedure of the same name. It has a round, deep bowl and an arched handle, similar to some bouillon or gumbo spoons. That deep bowl is the point: it holds a repeatable, consistent volume of liquid so every sample you taste across a flight of cups is drawn in the same quantity. Most cupping spoons hold approximately one tablespoon of liquid and are around 16 cm long.

The round bowl shape matters for technique, not just capacity. When you slurp from a round, cupped bowl, the liquid fans out laterally and hits your entire tongue -- tip, sides, and back -- in one motion. A flat or oval spoon narrows the stream and favors certain taste zones over others, making comparative evaluation less reliable.

Material is equally standardized. Each cupper has their own deep cupping spoon made of either silver or stainless steel to minimize flavor contamination. Silver was the historical preference, but stainless steel is now universal in professional and home settings because it's flavor-neutral, easy to sanitize between samples, and far more affordable.

## How is a cupping spoon actually used during a session?

*A question we hear often:* people who've watched cupping videos online see all the spoon handling and aren't sure what each step is for.

A cupping session involves the spoon at three distinct moments, and the purpose is different each time.

**1. Breaking the crust.** After ground coffee steeps in hot water for three to five minutes, the crust of grinds floating on top is broken with the spoon held perpendicular to the cup, with the cupper's nose close to the surface to catch the aroma. As you break the crust, there's something of an olfactory explosion as volatile aromatics escape into the air. This moment -- the "break" -- is one of the richest aromatic snapshots in the entire session.

**2. Skimming.** Immediately after the break, two spoons are used: one skims the foamy crust while the other lifts any stray grounds, keeping the surface clean. This "double spooning" technique also matters for hygiene at shared cuppings. You scoop coffee into a second spoon and taste from that second spoon, so the spoon that touches your lips never goes back into the shared cup.

**3. Tasting (the slurp).** Coffee is tasted with a quick, forceful slurp that aerates the liquid and spreads it evenly across the palate, sharpening perception of flavor, acidity, and mouthfeel. The audible noise is not affectation; it's function. The aeration volatilizes aromatic compounds and pushes them retronasally, the same way swirling works for wine. Tasting typically begins some eight to ten minutes after the infusion started, once the brew has cooled to around 71 degrees C. Tasting too early -- while the cup is still very hot -- suppresses sweetness and acidity perception.

## Is there a right way to rinse the spoon between samples?

*Readers frequently ask:* whether you really need to bother rinsing, or whether a quick wipe is enough.

Yes -- rinse, don't wipe. Before the cupping spoon goes into another cup, it's rinsed in a glass of water set aside for that purpose. Each cupper keeps a cup of hot water for rinsing the spoon and a separate cup of warm water for mouth rinsing between samples.

A wipe leaves residual oils and flavor compounds on the bowl. When you move from a fruity, natural-process Ethiopian into a clean, washed Kenyan, even a thin film of the previous coffee's oils will contaminate your perception of the next one. The effect compounds across a long flight. Professional cuppers rinse fastidiously, and at home -- where you might be comparing only two or three bags -- the discipline matters just as much, because the differences are subtler and harder to isolate.

## How is a cupping spoon different from a regular kitchen spoon?

| Feature | Cupping Spoon | Soup / Dessert Spoon | Teaspoon |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bowl shape | Round, deep | Oval, shallower | Oval, shallow |
| Bowl capacity | ~15 ml (1 tbsp) | 10-15 ml | 3-5 ml |
| Handle length | 15-17 cm | 15-20 cm (variable) | 12-14 cm |
| Material | Stainless steel or silver | Various (incl. plastic) | Various |
| Slurp performance | Even palate coverage | Narrow stream | Too small for tasting |
| Flavor neutrality | High | Variable | Variable |

The functional difference is real but modest. A deep bouillon or Asian soup spoon can substitute in a home cupping. The gap widens when tasting multiple coffees in a single session: a purpose-built spoon's consistent bowl volume means every sample is the same size, removing one variable from an already complex evaluation. When you're logging structured cupping notes -- the kind that let you compare a bag you opened last week to one you're opening now -- that repeatability matters.

## Do I need a cupping spoon to cup coffee at home?

*This one comes up a lot:* especially from readers who are just getting started with structured tasting and don't want to buy specialty gear before they know they'll use it.

No -- but having one makes a real difference. Cupping is an excellent way to quickly develop your sense of taste and learn what coffees you like best. You can use a deep, larger spoon as a substitute; the key is something that holds enough liquid for a proper slurp.

That said, a dedicated cupping spoon costs between $10 and $30 and will outlast hundreds of bags of coffee. The spoon is used daily in professional roasteries for selection and evaluation, and the same process translates directly to home use. If you're already keeping structured tasting notes (whether in a notebook or in an app like [Percolate](https://www.percolateapp.com), where you can log cupping notes against specific bags on your coffee shelf), investing in the right spoon reinforces the habit of consistency. One spoon, one rinse cup, one repeatable technique -- and your notes become genuinely comparable across sessions.

## What should I look for when buying a cupping spoon?

The goal is consistent sample intake: the bowl should tilt and hold liquid so every slurp draws the same volume. In practice, look for these features:

- **Stainless steel construction** -- durable, flavor-neutral, dishwasher safe
- **Round, deep bowl** (not oval) -- for lateral spread across the palate
- **~15 ml capacity** -- enough liquid for a full slurp without overflow
- **16 cm handle** -- long enough to reach the bottom of a standard cupping bowl without your hand hovering over the surface
- **Smooth finish inside the bowl** -- rough or patterned interiors trap residual coffee between sessions

Entry-level options from barista supply brands run $10-15 and perform identically to $40 professional versions. The La Cabra and Cafec spoons are popular in the specialty community; any stainless cupping spoon from an SCA-affiliated supplier will meet the standard. Avoid novelty "tasting spoons" marketed as all-purpose kitchen tools -- the bowl geometry is usually wrong for coffee evaluation.

## How does slurping work, exactly -- and why does it feel awkward at first?

Take a small amount of the coffee liquor onto your spoon and slurp. The slurping action doesn't need to be dramatic; it's really only meant to spray coffee across your palate to help you taste. For anyone who's slurp-shy, swishing the coffee around the palate can work in place of the standard noisy alternative.

The awkwardness fades quickly once you understand the mechanism. The sharp inhalation does two things at once: it aerosolizes the liquid so it reaches the soft palate and retronasally stimulates the olfactory nerve (where most "flavor" actually lives), and it distributes the liquid broadly enough that your brain registers acidity, sweetness, body, and bitterness together rather than in sequence. Most people who practice for two or three sessions stop noticing the sound and start noticing what the slurp reveals -- a brightness that was invisible when sipping, a finish that lingers longer than expected, or a dry, astringent edge that only shows up after the liquid is gone.

Tasting multiple coffees side by side makes small differences in flavor and expression more apparent, allowing comparison across origins and profiles. That's the whole point of the spoon: it gives you a standardized delivery system so the coffee -- not the technique -- is the variable.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I use a regular soup spoon instead of a cupping spoon?**
Yes, in a pinch. A deep bouillon or gumbo spoon is a reasonable substitute. The key is a round, deep bowl that holds about 1 tablespoon -- shallow or flat spoons spread coffee inconsistently across the palate and make comparative tasting less reliable.

**What size is a standard cupping spoon?**
Most cupping spoons are 15-17 cm (roughly 6-6.5 inches) long with a bowl that holds approximately 1 tablespoon (about 15 ml) of liquid. The SCA does not mandate exact dimensions, but this range is the professional standard.

**Do I need to rinse my cupping spoon between cups?**
Yes -- always rinse in hot water between each sample. Flavor carry-over from one cup to the next will distort your perception, especially when moving from a fruity natural-process coffee to a clean washed coffee. Keep a separate rinse cup ready throughout your session.

**What material should a cupping spoon be made from?**
Stainless steel is the industry standard because it is flavor-neutral, easy to sanitize, and durable. Silver spoons were historically used, but stainless steel performs equally well. Avoid plastic, which can retain odors and introduce off-flavors.

**How do I slurp correctly with a cupping spoon?**
Fill the bowl and bring it quickly to just below the lower lip. Use a sharp, audible inhale to spray the coffee forcefully across the full tongue and palate. The slurp also aerates the liquid, volatilizing aromatic compounds so your retronasal olfactory system picks them up -- the same reason wine tasters swirl before sipping.
