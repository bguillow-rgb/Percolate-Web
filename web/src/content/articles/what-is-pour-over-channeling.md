Pour over is supposed to reward patience. You grind carefully, heat your water, pour slowly, and yet the cup arrives tasting simultaneously sharp and flat, sour at the front and bitter at the finish. If that sounds familiar, channeling is almost certainly the culprit, and the fix is simpler than most brewers expect.

## What exactly is channeling in pour over coffee?

*A question we hear often:*

Channeling occurs when water carves one or more preferential paths through the coffee bed rather than percolating evenly through every gram of grounds. Water flows continuously through similar paths, divots, or channels inside the bed instead of spreading across it. In pour over, those paths form whenever part of the bed is denser, finer, or less level than the rest. Water follows the route of least resistance and largely ignores everything else.

The practical consequence: some areas of the coffee end up more extracted than others, producing a cup that is unbalanced in clarity and flavor. The channeled zone gives you harsh, over-extracted bitterness. The bypassed zones give you sour, under-extracted weakness. Both land in your cup at the same time.

It's worth distinguishing channeling from its close cousin, bypass. Bypass is water that finds its way into the final brew without passing through the coffee bed at all, generally by running down the filter wall. Channeling is water cutting *through* the bed unevenly; bypass is water skirting the bed altogether. They feel similar in the cup but have different causes.

## How do I know if my pour over is actually channeling?

*This one comes up a lot:*

You can catch channeling both during and after the brew. During the drawdown, look for:

- A single fast-draining spot in the bed, where one area empties noticeably quicker than the rest
- A ring of dry-looking grounds around the filter wall, meaning water bypassed the perimeter and concentrated in the middle (or vice versa)
- Bubbles or visible cracks in the bed mid-brew, which are air paths forming through the slurry

In the cup, the diagnostic signature is distinctive: simultaneous sourness and bitterness. The over-extracted channel contributes bitter notes; the under-extracted bypassed grounds contribute sour ones. That combination is rare in other defects, which is what makes it so useful for diagnosis.

A watery body paired with strong individual flavor notes is another cue. The brew feels thin overall, but the channel has pulled aggressively from the grounds it did contact. If your cup is consistently sour despite correct water temperature and ratio, and consistently bitter despite a grind that doesn't seem obviously too fine, check your bed prep before adjusting anything else.

## What causes channeling in a pour over brewer?

Four problems account for the vast majority of pour over channeling:

**1. Uneven bed prep.** An unlevel coffee bed before brewing guarantees uneven flow. The lowest point becomes a drain, attracting most of the water.

**2. Grind that's too fine.** Dense, fine particles pack together and force water to route around them rather than through them. Channeling often occurs precisely because the grounds lack enough permeability for water to move evenly.

**3. Aggressive or off-center pouring.** Pouring too hard disrupts the bed surface and drives grounds to the sides or creates craters. Every pour is an opportunity for channeling; slow, centered pours reduce that risk. Moving the brewer in a gentle circular motion while focusing on the bottom of the bed can break up existing channels and restore more even flow.

**4. Fines migration.** Fines, the tiny particles produced during grinding, punch above their weight when it comes to channeling. They clog filter pores when they migrate downward, slow flow at the bottom of the bed, create dense pockets that water has to route around, and pack into channels and reinforce them. A burr grinder with consistent particle distribution produces fewer fines and meaningfully reduces channeling risk.

## Does brewer shape make a difference?

Yes, and it's worth understanding before you assume the problem is purely technique. A conical dripper allows water to escape from the middle of the coffee bed more easily than a cylindrical one, which means bed unevenness gets amplified rather than smoothed out.

| Dripper Shape | Channeling Risk | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Conical (V60, origami) | Higher | Central drain concentrates flow; any bed unevenness is amplified |
| Flat-bottom (Kalita Wave) | Moderate | Multiple drain holes distribute flow, but corners can still trap dry grounds |
| Cylindrical / no-bypass | Lower | Walls prevent edge bypass; water is forced through the bed center |
| Wide flat with spiral ribs | Moderate-low | Ribs guide flow but can create ringed bypass if agitated aggressively |

This doesn't mean you should abandon your V60. It means channeling prevention matters *more* on conical drippers, and technique needs to be correspondingly more deliberate.

## How do I actually fix channeling in my pour over?

*Readers frequently ask:*

Work through this in order. Most channeling is solved by step two or three.

**Step 1 -- Level the bed before you bloom.** After loading grounds, give the brewer a gentle shake or tap to settle the bed flat. A Stockfleth's move (a flat finger drawn across the top of the basket) works well on wide flat-bottom brewers.

**Step 2 -- Adjust your grind coarser.** If your drawdown consistently finishes faster than expected in one spot, your grind is likely too fine for that brewer. Move one to two steps coarser, keep your dose and ratio constant, and watch whether flow becomes more uniform. Most V60 recipes target roughly 3:00-3:30 for a 15 g dose; times under 2:30 often point to either too coarse a grind or active channeling.

**Step 3 -- Pour gently and stay centered.** A gooseneck kettle helps enormously. The narrow spout gives you precise flow control. Start your bloom pour from low height, working in slow, tight circles. Keep subsequent pours centered and slow. Too much aggressive spinning can actually push water out to the sides and increase bypass rather than prevent it.

**Step 4 -- Check your grinder.** Blade grinders produce wildly inconsistent particle sizes and large quantities of fines that migrate and clog the filter. Upgrading to a burr grinder, even an entry-level flat or conical burr model, is the single highest-leverage equipment change for reducing channeling. According to specialty coffee industry data, particle size distribution from a burr grinder is typically three to five times more uniform than that of a blade grinder.

**Step 5 -- Consider your filter.** Paper filters generally restrict bypass better than metal mesh filters, which have larger openings. If you're brewing on a metal filter and struggling with channeling, try a paper filter for one session to isolate the variable.

If you log your brews in Percolate, you can tag bags with notes like "channeling at medium-fine" and compare drawdown times across sessions. That's useful for spotting patterns across different origins and roast levels.

## Is channeling worse with some coffees than others?

*A question we hear often:*

Yes, and the differences are meaningful enough to change your approach bean to bean.

**Fresh beans:** Coffee off the roaster within the first few days releases CO2 aggressively during bloom. That outgassing can create air pockets and uneven saturation in the bed, setting up channels before your main pours even begin. Resting beans 5-10 days post-roast reduces degassing and makes the bed more stable.

**Lighter roasts vs. darker roasts:** Light roasts produce denser, harder beans that grind into more irregular particles, often with more fines. Darker roasts are more porous and grind more consistently. Light roasts are statistically more prone to channeling and often benefit from a slightly coarser grind than you might expect given their brightness.

**Natural vs. washed process:** Natural-processed coffees tend to produce a stickier, more cohesive slurry during bloom, which can seal together and restrict even water flow. Washed coffees have a cleaner, more permeable bed. If you're switching between processing styles, watch whether your drawdown pattern changes.

According to SCA sensory training materials, even experienced tasters find channeling one of the harder brew defects to isolate, because it mimics both under-extraction and over-extraction simultaneously. Keeping tasting notes on each bag helps you correlate specific coffees with specific brewing problems over time.

## Does the SCA Golden Cup standard say anything about channeling?

The SCA's Golden Cup standard for certified home brewers specifies that the machine must deliver uniform water distribution across the coffee bed. Without even dispersion, water bores a single path through the bed, over-extracting that specific channel while leaving surrounding dry grounds untouched.

To hit the SCA Golden Cup standard at home, your brewer must operate at 195-205 degrees F, complete extraction within 4-8 minutes, and yield a TDS level between 1.15% and 1.35%. Channeling works directly against all three targets: it shortens effective contact time, drops TDS by leaving grounds underextracted, and skews extraction yield well outside the 18%-22% sweet spot. Preventing channeling isn't just a technique preference. It's the mechanical precondition for hitting the numbers that define a good cup.

## FAQs

**What does channeling taste like in pour over coffee?**
A channeled pour over typically tastes sour and bitter at the same time, sourness from under-extracted grounds that water bypassed, bitterness from the narrow channel that got over-extracted. The body also feels thin. This simultaneous sour-bitter combination is one of the most reliable signs of channeling.

**Is channeling the same in pour over and espresso?**
The root cause is the same: water finds a path of least resistance through the coffee bed. The consequences differ, though. Espresso channels at 9 bars of pressure and can be visually spotted through a bottomless portafilter. Pour over channeling is subtler, often caused by bed prep or pour technique rather than tamping, and is diagnosed mainly through taste and drawdown speed.

**Can grinding coarser fix pour over channeling?**
Often yes. Too-fine a grind creates dense pockets that water is forced to route around, opening gaps. Going one or two grind steps coarser improves bed permeability and encourages more even flow. If drawdown is still uneven after adjusting grind, check pour technique and bed leveling.

**What is the difference between channeling and bypass in pour over?**
Channeling is water cutting a path through the interior of the coffee bed, over-extracting that narrow zone. Bypass is water that skips the coffee bed entirely, usually running down the filter wall, and adds dilution without flavor. Both cause uneven extraction, but bypass tends to produce a weaker, watery cup rather than the sour-bitter combination of channeling.

**Does brewer shape affect channeling risk?**
Yes. Conical drippers like the V60 direct water toward a central drain point, which can concentrate flow and make channeling more likely if the bed is uneven. Flat-bottom brewers like the Kalita Wave spread flow more evenly across the bed, reducing, but not eliminating, channeling risk. Cylindrical brewers generally make it harder for water to escape via the edges.
