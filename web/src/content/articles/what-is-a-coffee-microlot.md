If you buy specialty coffee with any regularity, you've seen the word on a bag: *microlot*. It sits confidently next to a farm name, an altitude, a processing method. It sounds exclusive, and the price tag usually confirms it. But what does microlot actually mean, and is the premium worth paying? Here's a straight answer.

## So what exactly is a microlot coffee?

The term "microlot" gets used a lot in specialty coffee, and the definition shifts depending on who you ask. Generally, it refers to coffee traceable to a particular plot where the beans share the same climate, soil, altitude, and processing.

That last part, *the same everything*, is the key. A microlot is not just small. It's isolated on purpose.

At its core, microlot coffee is the most refined expression of single-origin coffee. A typical single-origin may come from a single farm, cooperative, or processing station, but a microlot goes further: one section of land, a limited varietal, or a tightly controlled harvest selection.

Think of it this way. A country-level single-origin might blend dozens of farms and thousands of bags. A regional single-origin narrows it to one cooperative or washing station. A microlot narrows it further still, down to a specific hillside, one varietal in one microclimate, or even a single day's harvest processed and dried completely apart from the rest of the crop.

Microlots are seasonal and produced in small batches, commonly from one or several producers, and they rarely exceed 20 bags of 30 kg each, with total batch sizes ranging from roughly 300 to 1,500 kg. That's a tiny fraction of what even a small roaster might buy in a year, which goes a long way toward explaining the price.

## How is a microlot different from just "single origin"?

*A question we hear often:* if the bag already says "single origin," why does "microlot" matter as an additional label?

The answer is specificity, and specificity is flavor. Microlot coffees tend to taste pure and distinct because the beans come from one small lot or were processed at the same washing station on the same day. A single-origin coffee can be a blend from a large region within the same country. A microlot is specific to one smaller lot.

Here's a quick comparison to make the hierarchy concrete:

| Lot Type | Traceability | Typical Volume | Flavor Consistency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial blend | Country or multi-country | Tonnes | Low - smoothed by blending |
| Single origin | Country / region / cooperative | Hundreds of bags | Medium |
| Single farm / estate | One farm | Dozens of bags | Medium-High |
| **Microlot** | **One plot, varietal, or harvest day** | **1-20 bags (30 kg each)** | **High - tightly controlled** |
| Nano-lot / project lot | Single tree row or experimental run | < 1 bag | Very high - collector level |

The further you go down that list, the more the cup reflects a single set of conditions. That precision is what you're paying for.

## Why do farmers bother producing microlots?

*Readers frequently ask:* isn't it just extra work for a small amount of coffee?

Yes, and that's the point. Growing and processing a small lot separately requires more time and expense from the farmer, so the goal is to grow better coffee that fetches a higher price. It's a deliberate quality bet: by isolating the best-performing plot or varietal on the farm, a producer can show what their land is capable of at its absolute ceiling and earn meaningfully more per kilogram for doing so.

Farmers typically reserve prime land with ideal altitude, shade cover, soil nutrients, and stable microclimates for microlot production. These plots often sit at elevated zones where cool temperatures slow cherry maturation, building stronger sugars and more concentrated flavor.

Harvest is almost always done by hand, selecting only perfectly ripe red cherries. Selective picking is labor-intensive, but it reduces defects, raises sweetness, and preserves cup uniformity, which are the qualities that define a specialty microlot.

There's also a relationship dimension worth noting. According to Union Terra of O'Coffee Brazilian Estates, microlot coffee is a vehicle for long-term relationships between producers and buyers, and it's more sustainable because feedback from buyers is easier to implement on a small scale. When a roaster sells your coffee under your farm's name, you have a direct reputational reason to keep quality high, year after year.

## Does "microlot" have an official definition?

*This one comes up a lot:* is there a governing body that sets the rules, or is it a free-for-all marketing term?

Honestly, it's closer to the free-for-all end. Microlot coffee is not well-defined in the specialty coffee industry. There isn't even consensus on the spelling ("microlot," "micro lot," or "micro-lot"). The definition shifts depending on who you ask, and without a shared meaning across the supply chain, the term can lose its value.

Microlots can mean different things to farmers, co-ops, exporters, importers, traders, and roasters. The label can refer to sustainably produced coffee processed with extra attention, a lot more carefully sorted and processed separately from larger lots, or coffees meeting specific production volumes, cupping scores, lot sizes, and prices per pound.

What this means practically: **always read the bag**. A legitimate microlot will come with specific provenance, including a farm or plot name, a harvest date or crop year, a processing method, and ideally an SCA cupping score. If the bag just says "microlot" with nothing else, treat it with skepticism. ## What do microlot coffees actually taste like?

*Readers frequently ask:* do they taste noticeably different, or is this mostly branding?

In a well-sourced microlot, the difference is real. Because every bean in the bag experienced the same altitude, soil, microclimate, and processing run, there's a consistency of character that larger lots can't match. Producers often go beyond standard washed, natural, or honey processing, experimenting with extended fermentation or special drying techniques to bring out unusual notes, such as double fermentation and sun drying.

Regions known for producing award-winning microlots include the Kenyan highlands, Central America's volcanic slopes, the southern Colombian Andean foothills, and Indonesia's high-elevation islands. Each produces microlots with distinct signatures. Kenyan microlots are often prized for piercing blackcurrant acidity; Colombian Andean lots frequently show clean tropical fruit or caramel-sweet balance.

Most microlots are specialty grade. Selection typically allows no more than 5 secondary and 0 primary defects per 350 grams of green coffee, resulting in SCA scores above 85 points. That's well above the 80-point specialty threshold, and elite microlots frequently land in the 87-92 range. ## How do I actually buy a microlot, and avoid getting burned?

A few practical rules for shopping microlots as a home brewer:

1. **Demand provenance.** The roaster's website or the bag itself should name the farm, plot, producer, and ideally the harvest year. "Colombia microlot" with no further detail is not a microlot. It's a marketing label.

2. **Check for processing details.** Washed, natural, honey, anaerobic, the method matters enormously for flavor. If you want to understand what those methods mean before buying, our [washed vs natural vs honey process](/washed-vs-natural-vs-honey-process) guide is a solid starting point.

3. **Look for a score or Q-grader note.** Legitimate microlots are cupped before purchase. A roaster who displays the SCA score is showing their homework.

4. **Buy fresh.** Microlots are seasonal and produced in tiny volumes. Once they're gone, they're gone. Check the roast date, and refer to our guide on [coffee roast date and freshness windows](/coffee-roast-date-freshness-window) to know exactly how long you have to enjoy them at their best.

5. **Log what you buy.** Microlots vary dramatically from roaster to roaster and harvest to harvest. Keeping a record of what you loved (and what fell flat) is the fastest way to sharpen your buying instincts. ## FAQs

**What is the difference between a microlot and a single-origin coffee?**
Single-origin coffee can come from an entire country, region, or cooperative, sometimes thousands of bags blended from many farms. A microlot is far more specific: it traces back to one small plot, one processing day, or one select varietal on a single farm, giving it a tighter, more defined flavor identity.

**Is microlot coffee always specialty grade?**
Not by definition, but in practice the extra labor involved in isolating and processing a small lot makes it economically rational only when the coffee scores high. Most microlots target 85+ on the SCA 100-point scale, and many exceed 87-90 points.

**Why is microlot coffee more expensive?**
Farmers must harvest, process, and dry a microlot completely separately from their main crop, requiring additional labor, separate tanks or drying beds, and careful quality control at every step. The limited volume (often under 600 kg) also means roasters pay a premium per pound to secure it.

**How do I know if a "microlot" label is genuine?**
Look for specific details on the bag or roaster's website: farm or plot name, harvest date or crop year, processing method, and SCA or Q-grader score. Vague use of "microlot" with no traceability details is a marketing red flag. Genuine microlots always come with provenance.

**Does a microlot coffee taste different from regular single-origin?**
Often yes. Because the beans come from one tightly controlled plot and processing run, flavor consistency is higher and distinctive notes are more pronounced. You're more likely to get the sharp florals, clean fruit, or unusual sweetness that makes specialty coffee exciting, rather than a blended-out average.
