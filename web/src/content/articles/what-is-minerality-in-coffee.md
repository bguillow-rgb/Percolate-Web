Minerality is one of those tasting terms that sounds authoritative on a coffee bag label but leaves a lot of people quietly puzzled. It's borrowed from wine, used fluently by Q-graders and competition baristas, and almost never explained in plain language. This guide fixes that: what minerality actually means when you taste it, where it comes from, and which coffees to seek out if you want to experience it directly.

## What does "minerality" actually mean as a coffee tasting note?

*A question we hear often:* bag after bag of specialty coffee uses the word "mineral," and yet no major flavor guide defines it cleanly.

Minerality in coffee describes a clean, inorganic sensation -- the kind of feeling you get from smelling wet slate, tasting a raw clay mug, or running your tongue along a smooth river stone. It is not the same as "earthy," which you might find in lower-quality or poorly-processed lots. Where earthy reads as muddy or damp, mineral reads as sharp and vivid -- almost electric in the best examples.

In practice, mineral character usually shows up as two things together: a textural quality (a slight dryness or grip at the back of the palate) and a flavor impression (something reminiscent of chalk, spring water, or calcite). It's more sensation than taste, which is partly why it's so hard to pin down the first time you encounter it. Once you've identified it in a cup, though, you'll recognize it reliably.

## Where does minerality in coffee come from?

Where a coffee grows shapes its flavor before roasting even begins. Minerality is among the most location-specific of all coffee flavors -- a direct expression of terroir. Three factors drive it most reliably.

**Soil composition.** Volcanic soils in regions like Ethiopia or Central America are high in silicate minerals, and the plant's uptake of those compounds influences how the cherry -- and ultimately the roasted bean -- expresses itself on the palate.

**Altitude.** Cooler temperatures at high elevations slow ripening, which preserves delicate aromatic compounds that read as mineral and floral rather than the heavier fruit flavors that ripen quickly lower down.

**Processing method.** Washed (or fully washed) processing removes the fruit pulp before drying, stripping away the fermentation-driven sweetness that could otherwise mask mineral character. A washed Kenya or Ethiopia lets the bean's origin voice speak loudest.

## Is minerality the same thing as the minerals in my brew water?

*This one comes up a lot:* people who've read about magnesium and calcium in brew water assume "mineral coffee" means the water is doing the work.

These are related concepts, but they operate very differently. Calcium, magnesium, and bicarbonate in brew water affect how much flavor you pull out of a bean and how you perceive it -- they're about extraction chemistry.

Minerality as a tasting note, by contrast, refers to a quality already present in the green bean, an intrinsic character derived from growing conditions, soil, and processing at origin. You're not adding minerality with your water; you're trying to coax out what's already there. That said, your brew water does interact with it. When alkalinity is too high, it reacts with delicate acids and the coffee tastes flat and bitter -- actively suppressing the mineral clarity you're after. If minerality is something you want to taste clearly, water in the SCA's recommended range matters. The Specialty Coffee Association's broadly cited starting point is a total dissolved solids target of 75-250 ppm.

## Which coffees are most likely to taste mineral?

| Origin | Processing | Mineral Character | Other Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kenya (SL28/SL34) | Washed | High -- bright, black-currant-meets-slate | Bold acidity, full body |
| Ethiopia (Yirgacheffe/Guji) | Washed | Moderate-high -- clean, spring-water quality | Floral, citrus |
| Rwanda / Burundi | Washed | High -- often described as "volcanic rock" | Red fruit, bright |
| Guatemala (Antigua/Acatenango) | Washed | Moderate -- volcanic terroir | Chocolate, stone fruit |
| Ethiopia (Sidama) | Natural | Low-moderate -- masked by berry sweetness | Full fruit-forward |
| Brazil (Cerrado) | Natural/Pulped Natural | Low -- nutty, chocolate dominant | Minimal acidity |
| Sumatra | Wet-hulled | None -- replaced by earthy/forest character | Heavy body, herbal |

Ugandan, Rwandan, and Burundian coffees tend to share a flavor characteristic that enthusiasts describe as "volcanic" minerality -- a term more common in wine but increasingly applied to coffee from these origins. Kenya earns a separate mention because its SL28 and SL34 cultivars, developed for high-altitude, nutrient-dense soils, produce a particularly vivid, almost saline-bright mineral expression that's unlike anything else in the coffee world.

## How is minerality different from "earthy" or "chalky"?

*Readers frequently ask* whether these terms are interchangeable. They are not, and the differences matter when you're building a flavor vocabulary.

**Mineral** is clean, refreshing, and intentional. It signals terroir and careful processing. Think: wet stone, chalk, spring water with a citrus edge.

**Earthy** typically signals soil or compost -- a warm, heavy, slightly musty quality common in Sumatran or aged Indian coffees processed by wet-hulling. It can be pleasant in small doses but is not prized the same way.

**Chalky** is almost always a negative. It describes a dry, coating, calcium-carbonate-like film on the palate that dulls everything else -- often a symptom of over-extracted coffee or water with excessive calcium hardness.

**Stony / rocky** is the most intense form of mineral character: compressed, almost dusty. Some competition-winning Kenyan lots go this far, and for enthusiasts chasing terroir, it's deeply desirable.

## Does roast level change how much minerality I taste?

Yes, significantly. Light roasting is the friend of mineral character, and in 2026 light roasts are no longer exclusive to niche cafes or competitions, which means more home brewers can actually experience minerality in beans that haven't been roasted past the point of recognition.

As heat increases during roasting, the Maillard reaction and caramelization create entirely new flavor compounds -- chocolate, smoke, bittersweet caramel -- that are far more intense than the delicate mineral compounds in the green bean. By the time you reach a medium-dark or dark roast on a Kenyan SL28, the mineral notes have been replaced almost entirely by roast character. The terroir is technically still there in the bean's structure, but you won't taste it.

If you want to experience what a coffee's origin actually tastes like, buy it as a light roast, brew it clean (pour-over or filter, not French press), and taste it without milk. The mineral character is most accessible in the first 15-25 minutes as the cup cools slightly toward 60-65 degrees C.

## How do I actually train myself to notice minerality?

The fastest method is side-by-side comparison, what the specialty world calls a coffee flight. Brew a washed Kenyan or Ethiopian next to a natural-processed Brazilian or a Sumatran wet-hulled lot. The contrast is instructive: once you identify what's *absent* in the heavy, earthy, fruit-forward cups, the mineral quality in the clean washed cup becomes much easier to isolate.

Beyond that, reference-tasting outside of coffee helps. Build up a library of sensory references. Smell a piece of limestone. Taste mineral water (San Pellegrino, Gerolsteiner, or another high-mineral brand) alongside filtered still water. Rub a piece of slate. These physical references give your memory something to attach the word "mineral" to before you encounter it in a cup.

Keeping structured notes accelerates this process dramatically. When you log the bag, origin, processing, roast level, and your tasting impressions together, the pattern of which coffees read as mineral versus earthy versus fruit-forward becomes clear over time. ## Frequently asked questions

**Is minerality in coffee the same as the minerals in brew water?**
No -- they're related but distinct. Brew-water minerals (calcium, magnesium, bicarbonate) affect extraction chemistry. Minerality as a tasting note refers to a perceived flavor quality in the bean itself, shaped by origin soil, altitude, and processing, not by what's dissolved in your kettle.

**What coffees tend to have the most minerality?**
Washed Ethiopian, Kenyan, Rwandan, Burundian, and some Guatemalan or Costa Rican coffees grown on volcanic soils at high altitudes are most frequently described as mineral. Natural-processed lots from the same regions typically express more fruit-forward notes that soften the mineral character.

**Is minerality a defect or a positive quality in specialty coffee?**
In specialty coffee, minerality is a positive descriptor -- a marker of clean, terroir-driven complexity. It's different from "earthy" or "musty," which suggest processing defects. A mineral note is crisp and stimulating, not muddy or funky.

**How do I train myself to recognize minerality in coffee?**
Side-by-side tasting is the fastest path. Brew a washed Ethiopian or Kenyan next to a natural-processed Brazilian or Sumatran. The contrast makes the mineral quality in the first cup much easier to isolate. Keep a tasting log -- apps like Percolate let you tag and compare notes across bags.

**Can roast level affect how much minerality I taste?**
Yes. Light roasts preserve the most origin-driven flavor compounds, including mineral character. As roast level darkens, caramelization and roast-driven flavors -- chocolate, smoke, bitterness -- progressively mask the delicate mineral notes present in the green bean.
