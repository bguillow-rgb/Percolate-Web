Setting up a home cupping session is the single highest-leverage thing you can do if you own more than one bag of specialty coffee at a time. It takes about 45 minutes, costs nothing beyond beans you already have, and teaches you more about your own taste in a single afternoon than months of casual drinking ever will.

## What exactly is a home cupping session, and why should I bother?

*A question we hear often:* isn't cupping just for roasters and Q graders?

Cupping is the globally standardized method for evaluating coffee quality -- the same procedure used by producers, importers, and competition judges everywhere. Roasters cup to evaluate green samples before buying, importers cup to grade lots, baristas cup to dial in recipes. But the reason home brewers should do it is different. At home, cupping is the fastest way to develop your palate -- faster than drinking espresso or filter -- because it strips away all brewing variables and lets you focus purely on the coffee itself.

That's the insight that makes cupping so useful when you're building a coffee shelf. Brew three bags through three different methods with three different ratios and you can't cleanly compare them. Cup them side by side in identical vessels with identical ratios and you can.

## What equipment do I actually need -- and what can I skip?

*This one comes up a lot:* people assume professional cupping requires professional gear.

It doesn't. A few small bowls or wide-mouthed cups, a deep-bowled spoon, freshly ground coffee, hot water around 200 degrees F, and a timer are all you need. Professional cupping spoons exist, but any deep spoon from your kitchen will do fine.

Here's the complete honest equipment list:

| Item | Pro standard | Home substitute | Skip entirely? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cupping bowls | 207-266 ml tempered glass or ceramic | Any same-size ceramic mugs or soup bowls | No -- same-size is important |
| Cupping spoon | Silver, 4-5 ml, deep bowl | Any deep kitchen spoon | Fine to skip the pro version |
| Scale | 0.1 g resolution | Any gram-accurate kitchen scale | No -- dose accuracy matters |
| Grinder | Calibrated burr grinder | Your everyday burr grinder | Don't skip grinding fresh |
| Kettle | Temperature-controlled gooseneck | Standard kettle, 30-second rest after boil | Gooseneck is nice, not required |
| Timer | -- | Phone timer | No |
| Notepad or tasting sheet | SCA form | Plain notebook or Percolate's in-app cupping log | Highly recommended |
| Spittoon | Yes, for long sessions | Optional bowl | Yes, for a 2-3 coffee session |

The one thing that genuinely can't be substituted: freshly ground beans. Coffee begins losing aromatic compounds the moment it's ground, so you want whole-bean coffee roasted within the past one to two weeks, ground immediately before your session.

## How do I pick which coffees to cup together?

*Readers frequently ask:* should I cup whatever I happen to have open, or do I need to plan?

Two coffees is the minimum -- you need contrast to learn anything. More than four starts to fatigue your palate and makes your notes unreliable. Three or four is the sweet spot for a beginner session. Pick beans with real differences between them, not just different bag art.

The most educational pairings for home cuppers:

- **Same origin, different process** -- e.g., an Ethiopian washed vs. an Ethiopian natural. This isolates processing as a variable and makes the flavor impact of processing unmistakable. (See our guide on [washed vs. natural vs. honey process](/articles/washed-vs-natural-vs-honey-process) for what to listen for.)
- **Same process, different origin** -- e.g., two washed single origins from Colombia and Kenya. Isolates terroir and variety.
- **Same roaster, different roast level** -- shows you how development affects flavor more concretely than any description can.

Use at least one coffee you already know you like as a base reference. That anchor makes every comparison more meaningful -- you're not just groping in the dark. ## What are the exact numbers I need for the ratio and temperature?

The SCA cupping protocol specifies every variable: grind size (coarse, approximately 8.5 on a Baratza Virtuoso or equivalent), coffee-to-water ratio (8.25 g per 150 ml), water temperature (93 degrees C), and steeping time (four minutes).

For home use, you don't need to hit these numbers to the decimal. You do need to be consistent across every cup in the same session. A medium-coarse grind, similar to a pour-over setting, works well. The most important thing isn't a specific number -- it's using the same grind size every time so your sessions are comparable.

No temperature-controlled kettle? Bring the water to a boil and wait 30-60 seconds. That gets you close enough.

## Walk me through the actual session, step by step

*A question we hear often:* what do I actually do once everything's set up?

**Before you pour:**

1. Set out your cups in a row -- one per coffee, labeled on the outside (or cover the labels for a blind tasting). Weigh 8.25 g of whole beans per cup and grind them one at a time directly into each cup. Run 5-10 g of each coffee through the grinder and discard it first, to clear any residual grounds from the previous sample.

2. Immediately after grinding, nose each cup. This dry fragrance phase is the most volatile part of the evaluation and disappears fast -- you have about 15 seconds. Note intensity and any qualities that stand out: floral, nutty, fruity, earthy.

**The pour and steep:**

3. Pour 150 ml of 93 degrees C water over the grounds in one smooth, even pour per cup. Start your timer immediately. Hover your nose over each cup right after pouring -- you'll catch aromatic compounds escaping as the grounds saturate. Assess the wet aroma within those first four minutes.

4. At exactly 4 minutes, break the crust: stir three times with your spoon and smell what's released. Rinse the spoon between cups. Skim off floating grounds and foam.

**The tasting:**

5. Let the cups cool for 5-8 minutes, then start tasting. Slurp from the spoon -- loudly. The slurp aerates the coffee and spreads it across your palate. Evaluate flavor, acidity, body, aftertaste, balance, and sweetness.

6. Come back at two or three temperatures. This is one of the most underused steps in home cupping. A cup that tastes flat at 70 degrees C often reveals citrus brightness or chocolate body at 55 degrees C. Many off-flavors -- ferment, paper, rubber -- only announce themselves when the cup is nearly cool.

7. Write something down after each pass. Even a single adjective per cup per temperature is enough to start. Over time those notes compound into a genuine taste map. ## How does a simplified home protocol compare to the full SCA standard?

The SCA protocol is the gold standard but overbuilt for casual evaluation. A simplified version -- one cup per coffee, consistent dose and temperature, four-minute steep, slurp at two temperatures -- gives you most of the diagnostic value with a fraction of the setup.

The main things the home version skips:

- **Five cups per sample.** The SCA uses five bowls per lot to check uniformity across the same bag. At home, one is enough unless you suspect grinder inconsistency.
- **Formal 100-point scoring.** You don't need to fill out an SCA form to benefit from cupping. If you want to understand what those scores mean, our [coffee cupping score explainer](/articles/what-is-a-coffee-cupping-score) breaks down the full SCA scale.
- **Roast-to-spec beans.** The protocol calls for a light-to-medium roast completed within 24 hours of cupping and rested at least 8 hours -- roughly 55-60 on the Agtron scale, intentionally lighter than most consumer roasts because lighter roasts reveal more origin character. At home, use whatever fresh beans you have and just note the roast level in your records.

## What should I actually write down during a session?

*Readers frequently ask:* do I need a formal cupping sheet, or will any notebook do?

Any notebook works. The goal is to capture something specific at each temperature pass -- not "tastes good" but "stone fruit at hot, brown sugar and low acidity at warm, slightly papery finish when cool." Specificity is the whole point.

A lightweight structure that works well: note **fragrance** (dry grounds), **aroma** (just after pouring), **flavor** at first sip, **acidity** (bright/muted/citric/malic), **body** (light/medium/heavy), **aftertaste** (how long, what character), and a one-line **overall impression**. This maps directly to the attributes on the [SCA coffee flavor wheel](/articles/how-to-use-the-coffee-flavor-wheel), which is worth having open during your first few sessions as a vocabulary reference.

Cupping even once a month measurably accelerates how quickly tasters learn to distinguish origin-driven flavors from process-driven ones -- the kind of skill that makes every subsequent bag you buy more rewarding. The [coffee journal guide](/articles/how-to-keep-a-coffee-journal) covers how to structure long-term records so those sessions build on each other rather than existing in isolation.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many coffees should I cup in one session?**
Two to four coffees is ideal for beginners. Fewer than two gives you nothing to compare; more than four overwhelms the palate and makes note-taking unreliable. Pick coffees with genuine differences -- different origins, processes, or roast levels -- so the contrasts are clear.

**Do I need professional cupping bowls and spoons?**
No. Any heat-safe ceramic cups or bowls in the 150-200 ml range work fine. A regular deep kitchen spoon substitutes perfectly for a professional cupping spoon. Consistency between cups matters far more than the specific vessel.

**What grind size should I use for cupping at home?**
Medium-coarse -- roughly the texture of coarse sand, similar to a pour-over setting. The important thing is to use the same grind size every session so you can make meaningful comparisons over time.

**How long should I steep the coffee before breaking the crust?**
Exactly 4 minutes. This is the SCA standard and produces consistent, comparable results. Start your timer the moment you finish pouring, then break the crust gently with the back of your spoon at the 4-minute mark.

**When is the best time to taste during a cupping session?**
Taste at two or three temperatures: first when the cup cools to around 70 degrees C (hot but sippable), then at roughly 55 degrees C (warm), and again near room temperature. Many flavor defects and subtleties only become apparent as a cup cools.

**Can I use pre-ground coffee for a home cupping?**
Technically yes, but it significantly degrades aroma evaluation -- the dry fragrance phase, which happens before you add water, is the first and most volatile layer of scent. For the best results, grind each sample immediately before the session, right into the cup.

**How is cupping different from just brewing and drinking coffee?**
Cupping removes all brewing variables -- no filter, no bloom, no agitation, no bypass -- so the only thing you're evaluating is the coffee itself. It's the same reason Q graders worldwide cup with an identical protocol: it makes cross-session comparisons meaningful.
