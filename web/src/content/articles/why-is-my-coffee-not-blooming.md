Most of us have poured hot water over grounds expecting that satisfying dome of bubbles and gotten nothing. No puff, no fizz, just wet coffee sitting flat in the filter. It's one of the most common frustrations in pour-over brewing, and it almost always has a fixable cause. Here's how to read what your bloom (or lack of one) is telling you, and exactly what to change.

---

## Wait - what is the bloom supposed to look like?

Before troubleshooting, it helps to know what you're aiming for.

Coffee bloom is the rapid release of carbon dioxide gas that occurs when hot water first touches fresh coffee grounds. You'll see the grounds bubble, swell, and puff up, almost like they're exhaling. This reaction typically lasts 30 to 45 seconds and occurs at the very start of brewing, particularly in manual methods like pour over, French press, and Chemex.

The bloom does two things. It's a technique: letting gas escape before full extraction begins leads to more evenly developed flavor. It's also a freshness indicator. A vigorous, foamy bloom signals fresh coffee with plenty of CO2 still trapped inside, while a weak or absent bloom often means the beans have already degassed, which usually points to older coffee.

If yours looks flat, faint, or nonexistent, one of the six causes below is almost certainly the reason.

---

## Why does my coffee bloom so weakly - or not at all?

*A question we hear often:* "I'm doing everything the same as before, but my bloom is nearly invisible."

Pour-over coffee stops blooming when the beans have been depleted of carbon dioxide (CO2) and gone stale. This happens when roasted coffee beans come into contact with oxygen. But staleness isn't the only culprit. Here are the six most common causes, roughly in order of likelihood:

| Cause | What you'll see | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Stale beans (>5-6 weeks post-roast)** | No bloom at all, or large air-pocket bubbles | Buy fresher beans; check roast date |
| **Pre-ground coffee** | Minimal puff, flat bed | Grind whole beans just before brewing |
| **Water too cool (<90 degrees C / 194 degrees F)** | Slow, weak bloom | Brew at 92-96 degrees C (197-205 degrees F) |
| **Grind too coarse** | Small bloom, quick drain | Go finer for pour over |
| **Not enough bloom water** | Dry patches, uneven bubbling | Use 2-3x the coffee weight in water |
| **Very fresh dark roast** | Modest bloom (not absent) | Normal - dark roasts degas faster |

## My beans are new - so why aren't they blooming?

*This one comes up a lot:* "I just bought a bag, it's still sealed, and the bloom is pathetic."

Two factors trip people up here. First, check the roast date, not the "best by" date. Coffee degasses more quickly in pre-ground coffee or in roasted coffee that is more than 5 weeks old. Many supermarket bags list a best-by date 12 months out, which tells you nothing about when the beans were actually roasted. A bag roasted nine months ago can legally sit on a shelf and still be "in date" but it will barely bloom.

Second, confirm you're buying whole beans and grinding them yourself. Pre-ground coffee blooms less than freshly ground because grinding accelerates gas loss. The moment you crack open the bean's cell structure, CO2 escapes into the air rather than staying trapped for your bloom. Even excellent, recently roasted coffee ground two days in advance will show a noticeably weaker bloom than the same coffee ground right before brewing.

The practical fix: look for a roast date on the bag (not just a best-by date), aim to brew within 2-6 weeks of roast, and grind to order every time. ## Could my water temperature be killing the bloom?

Yes, and this cause is underdiagnosed. CO2 escapes faster with hotter water. If your water is below 90 degrees C (194 degrees F), you're missing the degassing sweet spot. Aim for 92-96 degrees C (197-205 degrees F), not boiling, but close.

If you're eyeballing your kettle or letting water boil and then sit for ten minutes, you may be brewing significantly cooler than you think. A gooseneck kettle with a built-in thermometer or temperature control eliminates this variable entirely. ## Does grind size affect how well coffee blooms?

*Readers frequently ask:* "I switched to a coarser grind and my bloom disappeared. Are those two things related?"

They are. Big, chunky grounds mean less surface area, which leads to poor gas release. For pour over, you want a medium-fine grind, something like table salt. A coarser grind has fewer exposed cell surfaces in contact with water, so CO2 has fewer exit points and escapes more slowly or incompletely during the bloom phase.

That said, grind size alone rarely eliminates a bloom entirely. It tends to make a weak bloom weaker. If going finer restores only a marginal bloom, the beans themselves are probably the primary problem.

## I'm using the right amount of water, but the bloom is uneven - some spots puff, some don't. What's wrong?

This is where many people go wrong: pour in a gentle circular motion, starting from the center and moving outward, to wet all grounds evenly. Avoid dumping water in one spot.

Uneven saturation during the bloom leads to dry patches in the coffee bed, which then become channels during your main pour. Water rushes through the wet, already-extracted areas and bypasses the dry ones. The result is an under-extracted, weak cup even if your beans are perfectly fresh. A gooseneck kettle makes this circular pour much more controlled than a standard pouring kettle.

How much water matters, too. A good rule is to use 2-3 times the weight of your coffee grounds. For 20g of coffee, that's 40-60g of water for the bloom. Skimping on the bloom water leads to uneven extraction and coffee that tastes flat, sour, or overly bitter.

## Does roast level change how dramatically coffee blooms?

It does, and this distinction catches a lot of home brewers off guard. Light roasts hold more CO2 for longer and bloom more dramatically, sometimes rising like a muffin top. Dark roasts degas faster post-roast, so their bloom is often gentler, but still present and still worth doing.

This means a flat bloom on a dark roast isn't automatically a sign of stale coffee. It can simply be the nature of the roast. What you shouldn't see, even on a dark roast, is no bloom at all within a reasonable freshness window. If a dark roast that's only three weeks off-roast shows zero activity, check storage: if your beans were kept in a not-so-airtight jar, the CO2 has already escaped. No gas equals no bloom.

---

## What if my coffee hasn't tasted right lately - is a weak bloom the reason?

A weak bloom and a flat-tasting cup are usually symptoms of the same root cause: oxidation. Letting CO2 escape before brewing improves flavor in two ways. Carbon dioxide has a sour taste, so if grounds aren't allowed to bloom before brewing, the gas infuses a sour note into the cup. CO2 also repels water, which interferes with extraction. Water can only extract aromatics and oils freely once the CO2 has escaped.

So a weak bloom can degrade your cup in two directions at once: whatever CO2 remains creates sourness, while reduced degassing means water can't saturate the bed evenly, leading to under-extraction and flatness in the same cup. If you've noticed both sourness and a hollow, thin body recently, stale beans are almost certainly the cause.

Checking your roast date before brewing is the single highest-leverage habit in home specialty coffee. ## FAQs

**Is it bad if my coffee doesn't bloom?**
A weak or absent bloom usually means your beans have lost most of their CO2 and flavor compounds to oxidation. The coffee is still drinkable, but it will taste flatter than fresh beans brewed with a proper bloom.

**How much water should I use for the bloom?**
Use 2-3 times the weight of your dry coffee grounds. For a 20g dose, that's 40-60g of water. Too little water leaves some grounds dry and releases CO2 unevenly.

**How long should a coffee bloom last?**
Standard bloom time is 30 to 45 seconds. If your bloom is still aggressively puffing after 30 seconds, give it another 5-10 seconds. If it's already settled, start your main pour.

**Does decaf coffee bloom?**
Yes, but less vigorously. The decaffeination process removes some CO2 along with caffeine, so even fresh decaf tends to show a subtler bloom. Decaf also generally stales faster than regular coffee, given the additional processing involved, so freshness matters even more with decaf.

**Does dark roast coffee bloom less than light roast?**
Yes. Light roasts hold their CO2 longer and bloom more dramatically, sometimes rising like a muffin top. Dark roasts degas faster post-roast, so their bloom is often gentler, but it's still there and still worth doing properly.
