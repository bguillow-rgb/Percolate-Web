Coffee concentrate sounds like a specialty-cafe term, but it's one of the most practical things a home brewer can keep in the fridge. Make one batch on Sunday and you have a flexible base for a full week of drinks: iced lattes, quick hot cups, even baked goods, without touching your grinder again until Thursday.

## So what actually *is* coffee concentrate?

A coffee concentrate is a potent liquid brewed at a higher coffee-to-water ratio than a standard cup, strong enough that you'd dilute it with cold or hot water before drinking. That's the whole definition. "Concentrate" refers to strength, not method. It can be made with either cold or hot water, so not every concentrate is a cold brew.

Where most home brewers go wrong is assuming concentrate and cold brew mean the same thing. Cold brew describes *how* the coffee is made: ground coffee steeped in cold water for 12 to 24 hours. Concentrate describes how strong it is. A coffee can be both at once. A cold brew coffee concentrate is brewed cold and made strong enough to need diluting before you drink it. The terms overlap but don't define each other.

## What's the right ratio for making concentrate at home?

*A question we hear often:* people aren't sure whether to measure by volume or weight, or where to even begin.

Cold brew concentrate is typically brewed at a 1:4 coffee-to-water ratio and diluted before drinking. Regular ready-to-drink cold brew sits closer to 1:8 and goes straight into the glass. So if you weigh out 100g of coarsely ground coffee, you'd steep it in 400g (roughly 400ml) of cold water. That gives you a strong concentrate you dilute 1:1 with water or milk before drinking. Want something less intense, or plan to sip it more directly? Adjust to 1:6 or 1:8.

Grind size matters. Go coarse, around the texture of kosher salt or coarse sea salt, because fine grinds over-extract during a long cold steep and turn the concentrate harsh and gritty. A burr grinder is the right tool; the even particle size makes filtering dramatically easier. (If you're still using a blade grinder, our [burr grinder vs blade grinder guide](/burr-grinder-vs-blade-grinder) explains why that gap is especially costly for cold extraction.)

Here's a practical ratio reference for making concentrate at home:

| Brew Goal | Coffee-to-Water Ratio | Serving Dilution | Steeping Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| True concentrate (cold) | 1:4 | 1:1 with water or milk | 12-24 hrs |
| Medium concentrate (cold) | 1:6 | 1:1 or drink straight | 12-18 hrs |
| RTD cold brew | 1:8 | None needed | 12-24 hrs |
| Hot-brew concentrate | 1:4-1:6 | 1:1 or to taste | 4-6 min |
| Light roast concentrate | 1:3.5 | 1:1 with water or milk | 16-24 hrs |

Bean origin, roast level, grind size, and steeping time all interact with your base ratio. Treat 1:4 as a starting point, not a fixed rule.

## How is the flavor different from regular brewed coffee?

*This one comes up a lot:* home brewers expect concentrate to taste like very strong drip coffee, and are usually surprised when it doesn't.

Cold-extracted concentrate tends to be smoother and noticeably less bitter than hot-brewed coffee. The slower, cooler extraction pulls fewer of the compounds responsible for acidity and bitterness, so what you get is naturally sweet, often with chocolate and nutty undertones. The exact character depends heavily on the bean, but the general profile is rounder and calmer than drip.

Hot-brew concentrate takes a different path. It extracts in minutes rather than hours and produces a sharper, brighter cup with more acidity. Both approaches are legitimate; it comes down to what you want in the glass. For a quick weekday iced latte, a hot-brewed concentrate ready in under ten minutes is genuinely useful. For something to sip slowly over ice, the overnight cold extraction is worth the wait.

## Which beans should I use for concentrate?

*Readers frequently ask:* whether their go-to pour-over bean will translate well to concentrate format.

Start with freshness. Beans roasted within the past 2-4 weeks extract with noticeably more depth and sweetness. This isn't a minor point. Stale beans produce flat, papery concentrate, and freshness matters more here than in almost any other brew method because you're pulling a higher volume of compounds from each gram of coffee. If a batch tastes lifeless and your technique was solid, check the roast date before adjusting anything else.

For roast level, medium to dark roasts with chocolate, caramel, or nutty notes perform reliably. Their sweetness amplifies under high extraction and holds up through dilution. Light roasts need a slightly higher ratio to compensate for lower solubility, but they can produce a bright, fruit-forward concentrate that's genuinely interesting if you want something less traditional. ## How long does concentrate last, and how do I store it?

Undiluted cold brew concentrate keeps 7 to 14 days in the fridge when stored in an airtight glass container. Once you dilute it with water, plan to finish it within 3 to 5 days. Diluted with milk, you're looking at 1 to 2 days.

The single most important storage decision is whether to dilute the whole batch upfront or per serving. Keep the batch in concentrate form and dilute each serving in the glass. That way you get fresh-tasting cold brew through the entire two-week window instead of a deteriorating pre-diluted jar.

Container material makes a real difference too. Plastic absorbs odors and flavors over time and seals slightly less reliably than glass. Expect 7-10 days in a decent plastic container versus 10-14 in glass. If you're using plastic, label the date and drink it within a week.

## What can I actually do with coffee concentrate?

This is where concentrate earns its place in the rotation. It's more of an ingredient than a standalone brew, and it works across a wider range of applications than most people expect.

In practice, the most common uses are:

- **Iced latte:** Pour 60-90ml of concentrate over ice, top with your preferred milk. No espresso machine required.
- **Quick hot coffee:** Dilute 1:1 with hot water. The result is clean and full-bodied, closer to an Americano than drip coffee.
- **Smoothies and shakes:** A tablespoon or two adds coffee flavor without the bitterness of instant.
- **Baking:** Many coffee-flavored baked goods call for instant coffee, but concentrate is a direct substitute. Tiramisu, coffee brownies, anything like that; if you already have a jar in the fridge, it goes straight into the recipe.
- **Coffee cocktails:** Concentrate is a solid base for espresso martinis or coffee old fashioneds.

One practical note on caffeine: cold brew concentrate typically contains 1.5-2 times the caffeine of standard drip coffee at the same volume. Always dilute before serving unless you specifically want an espresso-strength hit. Most concentrate ratios call for 50% dilution, so factor that in before pouring a tall glass.

## What are the most common mistakes when making concentrate?

*A question we hear often:* why a batch turned out bitter, muddy, or disappointingly flat.

Three culprits behind most failed batches. The first is ratio drift. Go too light on coffee and the concentrate tastes like regular coffee after dilution. Go too heavy and it's undrinkably bitter. The 1:4 ratio is the proven starting point for a reason.

The second is filtration. Squeezing the grounds is the single fastest way to ruin an otherwise good batch. Let gravity do the work. Patience here pays off directly in clarity and taste.

The third is bean quality, already covered above, but worth repeating. Cold brew is particularly sensitive to freshness, and stale coffee produces flat, lifeless concentrate no matter how long you steep it. If a batch tastes off and your technique was right, look at the roast date before changing anything else.

## FAQs

**What is the difference between coffee concentrate and cold brew?**
Cold brew describes the brewing method: steeping grounds in cold water for 12-24 hours. Concentrate describes the strength, a high coffee-to-water ratio meant to be diluted. Most homemade cold brew is concentrate by default, but concentrate can also be made hot, and not all cold brew is strong enough to qualify.

**What ratio do I use to make coffee concentrate?**
Start at 1:4 coffee-to-water by weight (e.g., 100g coffee to 400g water). This produces a true concentrate you dilute 1:1 before drinking. Adjust to 1:6 for a lighter concentrate you can sip more directly.

**How long does coffee concentrate last in the fridge?**
Undiluted, 7-14 days in a sealed glass container. Once diluted with water, 3-5 days. Diluted with milk, 1-2 days. Always dilute per serving rather than the whole jar at once.

**Can I make coffee concentrate with hot water?**
Yes. A hot-brew concentrate at a 1:4 to 1:6 ratio brews in minutes and produces a sharper, brighter flavor. It's faster and more acidic than cold extraction: great for quick iced lattes, less ideal for slow-sipping over ice.

**Which beans make the best coffee concentrate?**
Medium to dark roasts with chocolate, caramel, or nutty notes translate best. Light roasts need a slightly higher coffee ratio to compensate for lower solubility, but can yield a bright, fruit-forward result. In all cases, beans roasted within the past 2-4 weeks make a measurable difference in sweetness and depth.
