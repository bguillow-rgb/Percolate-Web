Flat Bottom vs Conical Coffee Filter: Which Brews Better?

By Percolate · Published September 7, 2026

Quick Answer

Flat bottom filters produce a lower TDS brew with smoother, sweeter, more even extraction, often described as rounder and more forgiving. Conical filters drive a deeper, more focused flow, yielding higher TDS and brighter, more tea-like or fruit-forward flavors. UC Davis research confirmed tasters could reliably distinguish the two from identical beans.

Whether you brew on a V60, a Kalita Wave, or an everyday drip machine, the shape of the filter basket beneath your coffee grounds is quietly shaping what ends up in your cup. Most home brewers spend time dialing in grind size and water temperature but give zero thought to basket geometry. Yet UC Davis research showed that basket shape can hit flavor harder than grind size alone. Here’s what that means for your morning brew.

What exactly is the difference between a flat bottom and a conical filter?

The geometry is exactly what it sounds like. Conical filters are taller and tapered; flat bottom filters have a low, wide profile. A conical basket (think Hario V60) channels water downward through a deep, narrow bed that exits from a single large opening at the tip. A flat bottom basket (think Kalita Wave or most automatic drip machines) spreads the coffee bed across a wide, shallow surface with multiple smaller holes or a perforated plate beneath.

Professor William Ristenpart, director of the UC Davis Coffee Center, notes that most commercially available brewers have either a flat-bottomed or semi-conical basket: “roughly speaking, half of them are flat-bottomed and about half are semi-conical.” Despite that near-even split, for years there was almost no controlled data on which shape produced different results in the cup.

Does the basket shape actually change how my coffee tastes?

A question we hear often: does something as simple as the angle of a filter basket really move the needle on flavor?

The answer, backed by published research, is yes. Researchers at the UC Davis Coffee Center, working with the Specialty Coffee Association, ran controlled tests using identical coffee, grind size, and water temperature with filter shape as the only variable, and tasters could consistently tell the difference between brews made with different types of filters.

What’s more striking is how much it mattered. Basket shape made a more pronounced sensory impact than grind size. The only factor tasters found more impactful than basket shape was roast level. That’s a remarkable finding for something most brewers treat as an afterthought. If you’re already obsessing over grind settings but have never experimented with basket geometry, you may have been optimizing around the wrong variable.

What does a conical filter actually taste like compared to flat bottom?

This one comes up a lot: when researchers and experienced tasters describe the flavor difference, what words do they use?

UC Davis Coffee Center researchers found very different flavor profiles when an expert panel evaluated identical coffees brewed with flat-bottomed versus conical baskets. The panel noted more tea-like and floral flavors from conical baskets, and more smoky, tobacco-y, roasty flavors from flat-bottomed ones.

The TDS numbers support this. Cone filters produced slightly higher total dissolved solids, meaning stronger extraction, while flat bottom filters brewed coffee with lower TDS and a smoother, rounder taste. In plain terms: a conical filter concentrates extraction in a focused column of water, pulling out volatile aromatics and high-toned fruit notes more aggressively. A flat bottom spreads the water load across a wider, shallower bed, producing more even saturation and, in many cases, a softer, sweeter result.

For dark roasts specifically, the flat bottom boosted chocolate, cocoa, and woody flavors, whereas bitterness intensified with the semi-conical shape.

Specialty coffee enthusiasts in the conical camp argue that cone-shaped brewers produce a balanced, well-extracted cup because of the steep, continuous angle. Those who prefer flat bottom brewers say it produces a tastier cup that’s easier to achieve without specific pouring techniques. Both camps have a point. They’re describing different priorities.

If you’re brewing a washed Ethiopian or a Gesha and want to chase floral aromatics and citrus clarity, the conical is your tool. If you’re brewing a natural Brazilian or a medium-dark blend and want something consistently sweet without a precise pour, the flat bottom is more reliable.

Does the basket shape change how I should adjust my grind?

Readers frequently ask: do I need to regrind my beans if I switch basket shapes?

Yes, at least to dial in. A slightly finer grind generally works better for conical and semi-conical filter baskets than flat baskets: medium-fine for the former, medium for the latter. The reason is hydraulic. A conical filter exits through a single point, so water velocity through the bed is higher than in a flat bottom. To slow that flow and maintain contact time, you grind finer. A flat bottom’s multiple exit holes and wider bed geometry naturally slow the flow, so a coarser grind still achieves adequate extraction time.

Water tends to flow more quickly through conically-shaped filter baskets than flat ones. Conical baskets therefore need coffee ground finer, to increase the flavor the water picks up. Flat-bottomed baskets, through which water flows more slowly, need a coarser grind because the water already spends more time in contact with the grounds.

As a practical rule: if you switch from a V60 to a Kalita Wave using the same coffee and dose, open your grinder one or two clicks coarser as a starting point, then taste and adjust.

Should I buy a brewer based on basket shape, or does it matter less than I think?

Researchers declined to declare one shape necessarily “better” than another, given all the subjective factors related to taste and preference. The key takeaway is that when coffee, water, temperature, and flow rate all remain the same, the flavor profile of brewed coffee changes in a perceptible way based on filter basket geometry.

Basket shape is a real variable, but it’s a preference lever, not a quality hierarchy. Neither is objectively superior. Cone filters encourage deeper, focused extraction while flat filters promote a more even flow. It’s a subtle difference, not one you’ll notice in every cup, but enough that once you recognize it, you can use it to shape your preferred flavor.

The most practical move is to use basket geometry as one more data point in your brewing decisions: match the shape to the flavor profile you’re chasing from a given bean, rather than picking a side and ignoring the other. If you log your brews in an app like Percolate, noting the brewer alongside your cupping notes makes it easy to spot patterns in what you actually enjoy across multiple bags. That’s the fastest way to figure out whether you’re naturally a cone person or a flat bottom person.

What about pour technique — does that override basket shape?

To a degree. Pouring technique on a cone brewer has an outsized effect because the single exit point makes the bed sensitive to where water hits and how aggressively it flows. With a flat-bottomed basket, water hits the center, spreads out radially, and exits through holes in the middle. That flow pattern differs from a semi-conical shape where water hits the center but doesn’t spread as much radially.

This is why many baristas find conical pour-overs more expressive but demanding. Small changes in your pour circle, pour rate, or number of pours shift the outcome noticeably. Flat bottom brewers are more tolerant of inconsistent pouring, which makes them a better fit for beginners or anyone who wants a reliable daily cup without a precision ritual. If you’re still building your technique, starting on a flat bottom brewer and working up to a V60-style cone is a sensible progression.

FAQs

Does filter basket shape actually change the taste of my coffee? Yes, measurably so. A controlled UC Davis Coffee Center study — using the same coffee, grind size, and water temperature with only basket shape changed — found that even inexperienced tasters could consistently detect a difference between conical and flat bottom brews.

Which filter shape is better for light roast coffee? Conical filters tend to highlight the brighter, more tea-like and floral notes that light roasts express. If you’re brewing a washed Ethiopian or a floral Gesha, a V60-style cone often gives more aromatic clarity. That said, a flat bottom can be more forgiving if your technique or pour is inconsistent.

Which filter shape is better for dark roast coffee? UC Davis research found that flat bottom baskets boosted chocolate, cocoa, and woody flavors in dark roasts, while semi-conical shapes tended to intensify bitterness. For a smoother, sweeter dark roast experience, flat bottom is usually the better fit.

Should I use a different grind size for flat bottom vs conical filters? Generally yes. Conical filters have a steeper angle and single exit point, so water flows through a deeper bed more quickly — a medium-fine grind is typical. Flat bottom filters slow the flow by spreading it across a wider bed, so they’re more tolerant of a slightly coarser grind or varied grind settings.

What pour-over brewers use flat bottom vs conical filters? The Kalita Wave and most automatic drip coffee makers use flat bottom baskets. The Hario V60, Chemex, and Origami use conical or semi-conical shapes. The AeroPress uses a flat bottom basket as well, though its inverted immersion dynamic makes the comparison less direct.
