Why Does My Coffee Taste Sour? Causes & Fixes

By Percolate · Published September 22, 2026

Quick Answer

Sour coffee is almost always under-extraction: water moved through the grounds too fast, too cool, or in too small a quantity to dissolve the right flavor compounds. The most common fixes are grinding finer, raising water temperature, increasing brew time, or letting fresh beans rest a few more days off roast.

Sour coffee is one of the most common complaints home brewers bring to forums, and the fix is almost always the same category of problem: under-extraction. When water doesn’t pull enough of the right flavor compounds out of the grounds, the cup lands sharp, thin, and hollow, like biting into an unripe fruit. The good news is that every cause has a concrete solution, and most of them cost nothing to try.

Wait — is my coffee sour, or just acidic?

A question we hear often: these two words get used interchangeably, but in specialty coffee they mean very different things.

Acidity is a desirable quality. It’s the bright, juicy, citrus-like liveliness you find in a well-brewed Ethiopian Yirgacheffe or a washed Kenyan. Tasters actively seek it out; it’s scored as a positive attribute on the SCA cupping form.

Sourness is a defect. It’s the sharp, puckering, almost vinegary sensation that happens when a brew is under-extracted, when the first flavor compounds to dissolve (organic acids, including acetic and citric acid) dominate the cup because the brewing process stopped before dissolving enough of the sweeter, heavier compounds that balance them out.

The practical test: if your coffee tastes bright and lively and you enjoy it, that’s acidity. If it tastes sharp, hollow, and unpleasant, like something went wrong, that’s sourness. The distinction matters because the fix for sourness is always extraction-related, while “too much acidity” sometimes just means the coffee or roast level isn’t right for your palate. If you’re building your sense of what each tastes like, Percolate’s structured cupping notes can help you log and track the distinction across multiple bags.

Why does under-extraction make coffee taste sour?

Coffee’s flavor compounds don’t all dissolve at the same rate. Fruity organic acids are among the first compounds water pulls from the grounds — they dissolve quickly. Sweetness and body compounds (sucrose degradation products, Maillard reaction compounds) dissolve next. Bitter, dry compounds come last.

Coffee channeling and under-extraction both happen when brewing water finds low-resistance pathways through the coffee bed, producing simultaneous zones of over-extraction and under-extraction and a cup that tastes both sour and bitter. But in the straightforward under-extraction case, no channeling, just insufficient contact, sour wins because you’ve dissolved plenty of acid and not enough sweetness to counterbalance it.

The ideal extraction yield range according to the SCA is 18-22%. Fall below 18% EY and sourness is almost inevitable.

My grind is the problem, isn’t it?

This one comes up a lot: yes, grind size is the single most controllable lever for fixing sour coffee.

Coarser grounds have less surface area, so water moves through them faster and with less contact time. Less contact time means you extract the quick-dissolving acids before the balanced sweetness catches up. Grinding finer increases surface area, slows flow rate, and extends effective contact, all of which push extraction toward the balanced range.

The fix is simple: go one or two clicks finer on your grinder, brew again, and taste. Repeat until sourness resolves. If you’re brewing pour-over, also watch your total brew time — most V60 recipes target 3:00-3:30 for a 15g dose. Finishing at 2:15 means your grind is almost certainly too coarse. For espresso, a shot that runs faster than about 22 seconds at a 1:2 ratio is a strong signal to grind finer. Could my water temperature be too low?

Readers frequently ask: yes, cool water is a very common and often overlooked cause of sour coffee.

Water temperature directly controls how quickly different flavor compounds dissolve. Brew too cool and you extract the fast-dissolving acids efficiently while leaving the sweetness and body compounds under-dissolved. The result: sour, thin, bright in the wrong direction.

Per AeroPress guidance, approximately 80°C (176°F) is used for dark roast coffee and around 85°C (185°F) for lighter roasts, though most specialty pour-over brewing targets even higher, typically 92-96°C (198-205°F) for light roasts. If you’re letting your kettle sit for five minutes after boiling before you pour, you may be dropping into a temperature range that under-extracts light and medium roasts. A gooseneck kettle with a temperature control function removes this variable entirely. The detailed water temperature guide on this site covers optimal ranges for every brew method if you need the full breakdown.

Is my coffee sour because the beans are too fresh?

This is one that surprises new specialty coffee buyers. Freshly roasted coffee, pulled from the roaster within the last day or two, contains a large amount of dissolved CO2 trapped inside the bean. Brewing too soon off-roast can disrupt extraction, cause channeling in espresso, and create a muted or sharp flavor profile because of this CO2 release.

The flavor sweet spot for many specialty coffees peaks between 5 and 12 days off roast, though this varies by origin, processing method, and roast level. As coffee degasses through that window, you’ll notice real changes in acidity, sweetness, and body.

Natural and anaerobic-processed coffees often need more rest than washed coffees, sometimes up to two weeks, before they settle into a balanced expression. If you bought a bag dated yesterday and it tastes aggressively sharp, try waiting three to five more days before brewing again. The sourness will often resolve on its own. Brew it too soon and you’ll often taste sharpness or imbalance, not because something went wrong, but because the coffee hasn’t finished becoming itself yet.

What about my coffee-to-water ratio?

A question we hear often: using too little coffee relative to water produces a weak, under-extracted brew, and weak extractions run sour.

When your dose is low, each gram of coffee grounds is being asked to release flavor into more water than it can adequately saturate. The water fills its initial extraction demand (acids) and runs out of soluble material before reaching the balanced compounds. The SCA’s standard starting ratio is 60g of coffee per litre of water (roughly 1:16.7). If you’re brewing at 1:20 or beyond trying to stretch a bag, sour is a predictable outcome.

Try pulling your ratio back toward 1:15 or 1:16 and see if the cup firms up. You can also compensate slightly by grinding finer, but ratio and grind work together, so it’s cleaner to adjust one at a time.

Can channeling make espresso taste sour and bitter at the same time?

Yes, and this is the scenario that confuses people most, because you might assume sour and bitter can’t coexist.

When water finds low-resistance pathways through the coffee bed instead of extracting evenly, some grounds are over-extracted while others remain under-extracted. The over-extracted zones contribute bitterness; the under-extracted zones contribute sourness. The shot tastes unbalanced, muddled, and often both sharp and dry at once.

If the grounds in the basket are denser on one side than the other before you tamp, water will punch straight through the loose side. Uneven distribution is the single biggest cause of channeling in espresso. The fix is proper puck preparation: level distribution before tamping (a WDT tool helps significantly), an even and level tamp, and a grind that isn’t so coarse that large gaps form in the bed. The guide to espresso puck prep on this site walks through each step in detail. Once channeling is eliminated, adjust grind size and ratio for the final extraction balance.

Does a light roast just taste sour by nature?

This one comes up a lot: no, but light roasts are more unforgiving of extraction errors than dark roasts, which is why they get the reputation.

Dark-roasted coffee has already undergone significant chemical transformation in the roaster. Many of the acidic compounds that create sourness have been driven off or converted. A slightly under-extracted dark roast might taste flat or thin, but rarely outright sour.

A light roast retains far more of those organic acids in their original state. People sometimes mistake a properly light-roasted coffee for sour if the flavor feels grassy or underdeveloped, so the distinction matters. If a light roast is genuinely well-extracted, right grind, right temperature, right time, it should taste bright and complex, not sour. If it still tastes unpleasantly sharp after dialing in all the variables, it may be an underdeveloped roast, or simply a profile that doesn’t match your palate. Taking structured tasting notes over time (Percolate’s flavor quiz and cupping log can help) builds the reference points to tell the difference quickly.

FAQs

Is sour coffee the same as acidic coffee? No. Acidity is a positive tasting quality, the bright, juicy, or citrusy liveliness in a well-brewed cup. Sourness is a defect caused by under-extraction, where sharp, unpleasant organic acids dominate because not enough sweeter compounds were dissolved.

Can water temperature cause sour coffee? Yes. Brewing below about 90°C (194°F) extracts acids early but leaves sweeter, heavier compounds behind, producing a sour, thin cup. For most specialty coffee, a target of 92-96°C (198-205°F) balances extraction across all flavor groups.

Why does my espresso taste sour even with fresh beans? Espresso goes sour for three main reasons: grind too coarse (fast, channeled shot), dose too low, or beans roasted within the last 1-3 days that haven’t fully degassed. Try grinding finer one click at a time, and rest the beans at least 5-7 days post-roast.

Can brewing too quickly make coffee sour? Yes. Short contact time means water extracts sour organic acids first, the fastest-dissolving compounds, while leaving sweetness and body behind. Slow the brew by grinding finer, reducing turbulence, or extending steep time.

How do I tell under-extraction from over-extraction by taste? Under-extracted coffee tastes sour, sharp, thin, and hollow, like unripe fruit. Over-extracted coffee tastes bitter, dry, and astringent. If your coffee is both sour and bitter simultaneously, channeling is often the culprit: some grounds are under-extracted while others are over-extracted at the same time.
