What Is New Crop Coffee? Harvest, Freshness & What to Buy

By Percolate · Published September 18, 2026

Quick Answer

New crop coffee refers to green beans from the most recent harvest season for a given origin. Because green coffee is perishable, new crop lots are prized for their brighter acidity, livelier sweetness, and cleaner flavor. Past crop beans, typically more than 12 months post-harvest, taste flatter and woodier by comparison.

When you’re browsing a specialty roaster’s website and see a bag labelled “current crop” or “new crop arrival,” that phrase is doing real work. It tells you something meaningful about the quality and freshness of what’s inside. Yet most home brewers never learn what it means, or why it should influence what they buy. Here’s the full picture.

What does “new crop” actually mean?

The idea that a particular crop is available and fresh for only a portion of the year is now long-ingrained in specialty coffee conversations. “New crop” simply means the green (unroasted) beans inside the bag came from the most recent harvest of that origin. Green coffee’s freshness depends not just on its immediate harvest timeline, but also on how long processing took, the duration of international shipping, and the extended (but far from infinite) window during which green coffee waits to be roasted. Sourcing from the newest available harvest matters for all of those reasons.

“Past crop” (also written as “old crop”) is the opposite: beans from a previous harvest season sitting in storage, either at origin or in an import warehouse. Older coffee has less sweetness, less acidity, and less body than fresher coffee. Depending on how old it is, it can taste like jute bags, cardboard, or dry wood.

How long does green coffee actually stay fresh?

A question we hear often: people assume that because coffee sits in unopened bags, it must last a long time. The roast date matters, but so does what happened before roasting.

Green coffee stays fresh for much longer than roasted coffee. Most specialty roasters put the window at six to twelve months after harvest and processing. Roasted coffee, by contrast, begins losing volatile aromatics within days of leaving the drum. The roasting process traps gases inside the beans, mainly carbon dioxide, and those gases help roasted coffee stay fresh briefly before diffusing out over days and weeks. Green beans are simply more stable.

The takeaway: a roaster working with 14-month-old past-crop beans will always be fighting an uphill battle against stale flavor, no matter how skilled they are at the drum.

Why does harvest timing vary so much by country?

This one surprises a lot of people. Coffee is an annual crop in most of the world, but “annual” looks very different depending on which side of the equator you’re on.

In the northern hemisphere, the coffee harvest typically runs from late winter to early summer. In the southern hemisphere, it falls from late summer to early winter. Some producing countries, Colombia and Sumatra among them, can have two harvests per year because of how their geography straddles different growing zones. Most countries have a single season that lasts several months, during which cherries ripen and are picked.

That variation means “new crop” from Ethiopia might land on US roaster shelves in March, while new crop from Guatemala arrives in August. Specialty importers track these windows carefully, and many roasters publish their current crop arrival dates so buyers can plan purchases around peak freshness.

How does crop age affect what’s actually in the cup?

Readers frequently ask: does it really make a detectable difference, or is “new crop” just roaster marketing?

It’s real, and measurable. Unlike commercial blends, which seek consistency, specialty coffee can vary noticeably from harvest to harvest, reflecting the environmental conditions of each year. But beyond year-to-year variation, the age of the green bean itself changes the flavor profile in predictable ways.

Fresh green coffee retains higher concentrations of organic acids, sugars, and aromatic compounds that translate to brightness, sweetness, and complexity in the cup. As green beans age past their freshness window, those compounds degrade. The result is a cup that tastes muted, flat, or papery, sometimes described as “old wood” or “burlap.” For coffees designed to showcase delicate fruit or floral notes (think Ethiopian natural or Kenyan washed), stale green coffee makes those descriptors essentially unreachable, no matter how skilled the roast.

The perfect cup of coffee begins long before the beans hit your grinder, starting in the field, where harvest timing can make or break the flavor in your morning brew. Quality established at harvest can only be preserved, never recovered, once the window closes.

Is there any coffee that’s intentionally aged?

This one comes up a lot: if fresh is better, why do some roasters sell “aged” coffee?

Yes, intentionally aged coffee is a real and legitimate category, and it’s the main exception to the new-crop-is-best rule. Aging techniques have been used for centuries in countries like India and Sumatra. One of the most prominent examples is Monsoon Malabar, which exposes harvested coffee to monsoon rain and winds along the Malabar coast for three to four months, producing a heavier body with earthy, spicy flavors and much lower acidity.

It’s worth keeping these cultural differences in mind: “aged coffee” doesn’t always indicate lower quality. Roasted coffee, though, cannot be intentionally aged the way green coffee can through dedicated processing techniques.

The rule of thumb: if the origin and process are designed around aging (like Monsoon Malabar), the age is a stylistic choice. If a bag is simply old-crop Ethiopian washed with no intentional aging process, the age is a defect, not a feature.

How do I know if I’m buying new crop or past crop coffee?

Most quality-focused specialty roasters are transparent about this, though the vocabulary varies:

  • “New crop arrival” or “current crop” is the clearest signal, indicating the lot was sourced from the latest harvest.
  • A specific harvest year on the label (for example, “2025/26 crop”) lets you calculate freshness yourself.
  • No crop information at all is a yellow flag; it’s worth contacting the roaster to ask.
  • An import date or “arrival date” on the roaster’s website is used by many transparency-focused roasters to show exactly when green coffee landed in their country.

Knowing what “new crop” means turns a marketing phrase into an actual buying decision. That’s the kind of practical language more coffee drinkers are looking for.

Does new crop coffee need more rest before brewing?

This is a subtler question than it sounds. Very fresh green coffee, once roasted, can sometimes produce a slightly grassy or underdeveloped cup during the first few days post-roast. That’s normal degassing behavior. Our full guide on how long to rest coffee after roasting covers the rest window by roast level.

The core point: new crop coffee needs its standard post-roast rest, but it does not need extended aging. Once rested, it will outperform past-crop coffee of the same origin at every stage of the cup.

What about harvest timing and buying subscriptions?

A question we hear often: if you buy a coffee subscription, how can you be sure you’re getting new crop beans?

This is one of the strongest arguments for choosing a roaster with transparent sourcing. According to the Specialty Coffee Association, global specialty coffee consumption continues growing at 8-10% annually, outpacing commodity coffee growth, and part of that growth is coming from consumers who expect more information about what’s in the bag. Reputable subscription roasters publish their current green coffee inventory, note harvest years, and rotate origins so you consistently receive beans from active crop windows. Our full breakdown of how to choose a specialty coffee subscription includes crop transparency as one of the key filters to apply.

The short version: ask any subscription roaster when their current Ethiopia or Kenya lot was harvested. If they can’t tell you, that’s your answer.

Frequently asked questions

What does ‘new crop’ mean on a coffee bag? It means the green beans used were sourced from the most recent harvest of that origin country, usually within the past 6-12 months. It signals freshness at the green-coffee stage, before roasting even begins.

How long does green coffee stay fresh? Most specialty roasters consider green (unroasted) coffee fresh for 6 to 12 months after harvest and processing. After that, flavor compounds degrade and the cup can taste woody, flat, or papery.

What is past crop coffee? Past crop (or “old crop”) refers to green beans from a previous harvest season, typically more than 12 months post-harvest. They’re not always undrinkable, but they lack the brightness and sweetness of fresh green coffee and often taste dull or cardboard-like.

Which countries have two coffee harvests per year? Colombia and certain parts of Sumatra (Indonesia) can produce two harvests annually because their geography straddles different growing zones. This means new crop Colombian coffee is more frequently available than single-harvest origins like Ethiopia or Kenya.

Does new crop always mean better coffee? Usually, yes, but not always. Intentionally aged coffees like India’s Monsoon Malabar are a deliberate exception, prized for their earthy, low-acid character. For specialty lots meant to showcase brightness and fruit, however, new crop is almost always preferable.
