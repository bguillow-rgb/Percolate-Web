Why Do Coffee Cuppers Slurp? The Science Explained

By Percolate · Published September 29, 2026

Quick Answer

Coffee cuppers slurp because the forceful aspiration aerosolizes the liquid across the entire palate, simultaneously triggering every taste receptor and pushing volatile aromatic compounds retronasally toward the olfactory bulb. This gives a far richer, more accurate read on flavor, acidity, body, and finish than a quiet sip ever could.

Specialty coffee professionals are famously loud at the cupping table, and first-timers often find the room overwhelming: a chorus of aggressive, wet slurps ricocheting off every surface. It looks theatrical. It sounds chaotic. But every one of those slurps is doing precision sensory work, and once you understand the mechanics, you’ll never go back to quiet sipping when you’re actually trying to taste a coffee.

”Okay but why are they slurping in the first place — can’t they just sip normally?”

A question we hear often: the slurp looks performative, but it’s the most functional move in a cupper’s toolkit.

Slurping is not a tradition or a quirky professional habit. It’s a tool. When a cupper scoops coffee with a cupping spoon and sharply slurps it, the liquid enters the mouth together with a large volume of air, which breaks it into tiny droplets and spreads it evenly across the entire palate.

That distribution matters because taste receptors are not evenly spread on the tongue. Sweetness tends to register toward the tip, bitterness toward the back, acidity on the sides. By aspirating the coffee across the palate, you hit as many of those zones as possible in a single pass. It’s the same principle as aerating wine.

A quiet sip, by contrast, delivers liquid mostly to the front of the mouth and lets it roll passively toward the throat. You’ll catch some of the flavor, but miss plenty — especially anything that registers at the back palate or through the nose.

”What does ‘retronasal’ mean and why does everyone keep saying it matters for tasting?”

This one comes up a lot. Your sense of taste is really two systems working together: orthonasal smell (what you detect by sniffing, before the coffee enters your mouth) and retronasal smell (aromatic compounds that travel from the back of your throat up to your olfactory bulb after liquid is in your mouth). The retronasal route accounts for the majority of what we experience as “flavor.” The distinction between a berry note and a stone fruit note, for example, isn’t something your tongue can make alone.

Slurping aerates the coffee and spreads it across the palate at once, pushing aromatic compounds up into the nasal passage where most flavor perception actually happens. Oxygen also helps release volatile aromatic compounds more quickly, which makes both positive characteristics and potential defects easier to detect. That combined evaluation is the whole point: a cupping is meant to give you a complete read on a coffee, not just “is this sweet or bitter?"

"How loud should a proper slurp actually be — is louder better?”

For anyone just starting out, the noise level at a cupping table can be more than a little startling — especially when everyone around you is slurping loud enough to hail a cab. But there’s a reason for it. A more aggressive slurp draws in more air, which means more aerosolization and better palate coverage.

That said, there’s a ceiling. Taking too large a spoonful makes it harder to slurp aggressively and raises the odds that you’ll inhale and choke. Keep the scoop small — roughly half to two-thirds of a standard teaspoon of liquid — and let the airspeed do the work. The goal is a fine mist landing on every surface of the mouth, not a gulp.

During slurping, the coffee mixes with air and cools slightly before it reaches the tongue. That’s a useful side effect when you’re tasting coffee that came straight off a near-boiling brew, since heat suppresses the perception of acidity and delicate fruity notes.

”Does when you slurp during a cupping change what you’re able to taste?”

Readers frequently ask: yes, and timing across the temperature curve is half the skill. Flavors become more pronounced as coffee cools, which is why the SCA’s standard cupping protocol (used by Q Graders worldwide) builds in multiple tasting passes over roughly 15-20 minutes.

A coffee that reads flat and generically roasty at 70 degrees C may reveal a clean raspberry acidity or a brown sugar sweetness at 50 degrees C that you’d miss entirely if you tasted once and moved on. More acidity is released as the temperature drops, and that changes the flavor noticeably. According to industry standards used by the Coffee Quality Institute, professional Q Graders taste each sample at hot, warm, and near-room-temperature stages before finalizing their evaluation scores.

This is also why specialty roasters and green buyers cup the same lot multiple times on different days. A single snapshot doesn’t give you the full picture.

”Should I spit or swallow? I’ve seen professionals doing both.”

Professional cuppers spit when evaluating many samples in one session to avoid caffeine overload. Home cuppers usually swallow. Neither approach affects the accuracy of your tasting.

Spitting is a practical necessity in a commercial quality-control session where a single cupper might work through 20-40 samples in one morning. At home with two or three bags — the kind of multi-bag setup that gets logged in Percolate’s coffee shelf — swallowing is completely fine and doesn’t compromise your read on the coffee.

One hygiene note that became standard after 2020: pouring your spoonful into a small intermediary vessel before slurping has grown more common at shared cupping tables, rather than dipping the spoon back into the communal bowl. Worth doing.

”How do I actually practice the slurp technique at home if I’ve never done it?”

This one comes up a lot: the mechanics feel awkward at first, but they click quickly with repetition. Here’s a simple at-home progression:

  1. Start with water. Fill a teaspoon and practice the slurp motion until you can atomize the liquid without choking. You’ll feel it hit the roof of your mouth and the back of your throat — that’s correct.
  2. Move to your morning coffee. Before your first sip from the mug, dip a spoon, hold it just at your lower lip, and slurp fast with a sharp inhalation. The goal is to spray the coffee across your entire palate, which engages all your taste buds and sends aromatic compounds up to your nasal passages for a much fuller read on the flavor.
  3. Name what you taste. Even rough language (“lemony,” “chocolatey,” “kind of drying at the end”) builds your flavor vocabulary fast. Apps like Percolate provide structured tasting note fields — acidity, body, flavor, aftertaste — that give you prompts if you’re stuck on words.
  4. Taste again as it cools. Come back to the same cup 10 minutes later and slurp again. Notice what changed.

With consistent practice, the slurp becomes second nature. The more flavors and aromas you consciously analyze, the deeper your sensory memory gets. Over time you’ll start catching things that once seemed invisible: delicate floral aromas, a faint stone fruit sweetness, or how the aftertaste shifts from one minute to the next. That’s the compounding payoff of treating each cup as a tasting rather than a caffeine delivery.

”Is there a difference between a cupping spoon and a regular spoon for slurping?”

Tasting is usually done with an especially round and deep spoon, from which coffee is slurped with gusto in order to spray as much of the coffee around the taster’s palate as possible. The bowl depth is specifically designed to hold a slurpable volume (roughly 5-8 ml) without spilling when angled at the lip. Cupping spoons are deeper and rounder than standard flatware, which helps maintain a consistent dose from sample to sample.

For home use, a standard teaspoon is a perfectly workable substitute. A soup spoon holds slightly too much volume, which makes aggressive aerosolization harder without overflow. If you want to invest: cupping spoons from brands like Comandante, Saint Anthony Industries, or generic stainless options from SCA-affiliated suppliers run $8-$25 and last indefinitely. They’re a minor upgrade that makes a meaningful difference in slurp control.

Frequently asked questions

Is slurping coffee the same as sipping it loudly? No. A sip delivers liquid to the front of your mouth. A proper cupping slurp forcefully aspirates the coffee so it’s aerosolized and sprayed across the entire palate — roof of the mouth, back of the tongue, and throat — while simultaneously pushing aromas retronasally. The loud noise is a byproduct of the air intake required to achieve that distribution.

Do I need a special spoon to slurp coffee at home? A proper cupping spoon is round and relatively deep, which holds just enough coffee for one clean slurp without overflow. In a pinch, a standard teaspoon or soup spoon works well. The spoon shape matters less than the slurping action itself.

Why does coffee taste different when it cools during a cupping? Heat suppresses certain flavor compounds, particularly acids. As coffee cools from around 70 degrees C to 40 degrees C or below, those acids become more pronounced and sweeter or fruitier top notes open up. Professional cuppers taste each sample multiple times as it cools specifically to catch this evolution.

Should I spit or swallow when practicing slurping at home? Either is fine for flavor evaluation — spitting doesn’t affect accuracy. Professional cuppers typically spit when evaluating dozens of samples in a single session to avoid caffeine overload. At home with two or three coffees, swallowing is perfectly normal and won’t compromise your tasting.

Can slurping help me get more out of a coffee I brew every day, not just during formal cuppings? Yes. Taking even one deliberate slurp from a spoon before your first sip engages far more of your sensory system than drinking straight from the cup. Over time this trains your palate to detect subtler notes — useful for logging tasting notes in an app like Percolate, where detailed flavor records help build your personal taste profile across every bag you brew.
