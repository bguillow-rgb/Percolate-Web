What Is Past Crop Coffee? Freshness, Flavor & What to Buy

By Percolate · Published September 27, 2026

Quick Answer

Past crop coffee is green (unroasted) coffee held over from a previous harvest year. Once the next crop lands, last year's beans are labeled 'past crop.' Flavor typically fades, losing brightness, acidity, and sweetness, though well-stored beans can still cup well for up to 12 months post-harvest.

Think of green coffee the way you think of seasonal produce. A strawberry picked in June and a strawberry that sat in cold storage through October are technically the same fruit, but they are not the same experience. Past crop coffee is the specialty industry’s shorthand for that gap between peak and fade. If you’ve ever bitten into a bag that tasted flat despite a recent roast date, past-crop green beans may be the culprit hiding behind that label.

What does “past crop” actually mean?

A question we hear often: The terminology can feel like roaster jargon at first.

By the time the next year’s harvest arrives, the previous year’s coffee is known as “past crop.” It’s still useable, but it’s from a different season, a different year. In practical terms, the clock starts from the point the green coffee is processed and ready for export at origin. Most specialty roasters consider green coffee fresh for six to twelve months; once that year mark has passed and the next harvest lands, the coffee becomes “past crop,” a label generally associated with cereal or aged flavor notes.

It is worth being clear about what “past crop” describes: the green, unroasted bean, not the roasted coffee in your bag. By the time you open a bag at home, the beans have already moved through harvest, processing, export, import, warehouse storage, and the roast itself, a pipeline that can take anywhere from three months to well over a year. The roast date printed on the bag tells you when the roaster fired the beans, not how old the green coffee was when it went in.

What does past crop coffee actually taste like in the cup?

For specialty roasters, freshness is tied to nuanced flavor characteristics that do not last forever. Bright citrusy acidity, sweet sugar-browning notes, lush ripe-berry flavors, and floral aromas all fade over time. Stale or past-crop coffees can taste papery, woody, or even faintly cucumber-like.

In general, older past-crop coffee has less sweetness, less acidity, and less body than fresher coffee. That’s not a subtle difference for a specialty drinker. Sweetness, acidity, and body are the pillars the SCA cupping form is built around, and when those fade, you’re left with a cup that tastes dull regardless of how carefully you brew it. Even a perfect setup with a great grinder and flawless technique produces a flat result if the beans have nothing worth extracting. The variable most home brewers underestimate is the bean itself.

How is past crop different from “new crop” or “fresh crop”?

This one comes up a lot: The specialty industry uses several harvest-season labels, and they’re easy to conflate.

TermWhat It MeansTypical Age After Harvest
New crop / fresh cropCurrent season’s harvest, recently landed0-6 months
Current cropStill within the accepted freshness window6-12 months
Past cropPrevious harvest year’s green coffee12+ months
Old cropBeans two or more harvests back24+ months
Aged coffee (intentional)Deliberately held and conditioned — e.g., Monsoon MalabarVaries; done on purpose

Fresh-crop coffee’s flavor is bright, the color is vivid, and it is at its peak. Over the coming months, those properties fade. A washed coffee moves from vivid grey-green to pale beige. Flavors mute, aromas become less lively, and the coffee seems less exciting overall.

Aged coffee, the intentional category at the bottom of that table, is a different concept entirely. Some coffees, such as Monsoon Malabar and Old Java, are past crop by definition. The harvested beans are aged by exposing them to monsoon rain and winds for up to four months, which causes them to swell and lose their original acidity, resulting in a flavor profile with a neutral pH balance. That’s a deliberate choice for a specific style. It is not the same thing as coffee that quietly aged in a warehouse.

Is past crop coffee always worse?

Readers frequently ask: whether it’s worth avoiding, or whether “past crop” is just a scare label.

The honest answer is that it depends on how the green coffee was stored. Cool, dry environments help preserve a green coffee’s freshness, and deep-freezing very nearly suspends its aging altogether. Packaging in special plastic or mylar bags helps too, likely by excluding oxygen. Specialty roasters spend significant money on climate control, specialized packaging, and careful inventory management to hold on to that fresh-crop character.

A growing number of people within the industry believe past-crop coffees can still be specialty. A coffee that scored in the mid-eighties when fresh may hold that quality for up to 12 months if stored correctly. Cupped a year later, it may perform just as well, or even better in some respects. Providing the coffee has retained enough of its flavor and aromatic compounds, it can still produce a great roast.

So the question to ask is not only “is this past crop?” but “how was it stored, and what did it score when it was fresh?” A mid-eighty-scoring coffee held in a climate-controlled warehouse in hermetic GrainPro bags is likely in better shape than a high-scoring coffee left in burlap in a warm, humid environment for eight months.

How can I spot past crop coffee as a home buyer?

Most bags give you very little to go on. Here’s what to actually look for.

Roast date alone is not enough. A roast date tells you when the coffee was roasted, not how old the green beans were when the roaster bought them. A bag roasted last Tuesday could have been made from green coffee harvested 16 months ago.

Look for harvest year or crop year. Some roasters list this on the bag or on a linked lot page. The crop year aligns with the origin country’s harvest season. Ethiopian coffees typically harvest October through January, while Colombian coffees have two harvest windows per year.

Look for arrival or landed date. Transparency-forward roasters sometimes list when the green coffee arrived at their warehouse. Cross-referencing that with the origin’s harvest season gives you a reliable freshness signal.

Ask. Most specialty roasters are happy to tell you the crop year of a specific lot. The generally accepted window for green coffee to be considered fresh is one year from processing and shipping, as long as it’s packed in hermetic packaging. Heat, light, moisture, insects, and air are the main factors that degrade green coffee. A roaster who can’t answer those questions probably isn’t tracking crop year at all.

If you’re logging bags in Percolate, the Notes field is a natural place to record crop year alongside roast date. That pairing gives you a much more complete picture of what you’re drinking than roast date alone.

Does the roast level mask past-crop flavor?

To a degree, and this matters for how you interpret your cup. Lighter roasts preserve more acidity and brightness. Darker roasts lower the acidity and bring out heavier body and roastier sweetness. A past-crop bean roasted dark can taste reasonably full and chocolatey because the roast-driven flavors cover the loss of origin brightness. The same bean roasted light will expose the faded acidity and flat sweetness far more clearly, since light roasting relies on the origin character to carry the cup.

This is one reason past-crop coffees show up more often in dark-roasted blends than in single-origin light roasts. The roast does some of the hiding. If you primarily drink light or medium-light roasts from single origins, you’re especially likely to notice the difference between fresh-crop and past-crop green beans, and especially likely to benefit from buying from roasters who publish crop-year data.

FAQs

What does past crop coffee taste like? Past crop coffee typically tastes flatter than fresh-crop coffee. Brightness and acidity fade first, followed by sweetness. Poorly stored past-crop beans can develop papery, woody, or cardboard-like notes.

How long is green coffee considered fresh? Most specialty roasters consider green coffee fresh for six to twelve months after harvest and processing. Beyond that window, it is generally labeled past crop, though well-packaged beans in cool, dry conditions can hold quality close to that one-year mark.

Is past crop coffee bad? Not necessarily. Well-stored past-crop coffee can still be usable and even cup above specialty grade. The concern is that its most exciting characteristics, floral aromas, citrus acidity, ripe-fruit sweetness, are typically muted compared to fresh-crop beans.

How can I tell if a bag of roasted coffee was made from past crop beans? You usually cannot tell from the bag alone. Look for a roast date and ask the roaster about their green sourcing. Roasters who publish crop year, harvest date, or arrival date on their bags or websites are giving you the clearest freshness signal.

What is the difference between past crop and old crop coffee? “Past crop” typically means green coffee from the previous harvest year that is still circulating in the supply chain. “Old crop” is a looser term sometimes used for beans two or more years past harvest, where flavor degradation is more severe.
