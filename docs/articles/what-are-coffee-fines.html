What Are Coffee Fines? How Tiny Particles Shape Your Cup

By Percolate · Published September 9, 2026

Quick Answer

Coffee fines are the dust-like micro-particles produced every time you grind beans, typically anything under 100, 200 microns in diameter. They extract faster than larger particles, which can add bitterness and astringency to filter brews, but they also contribute body and extraction efficiency in espresso. Whether to remove them depends on your brew method.

Every grind produces a range of particle sizes. Most land in a useful middle zone. But at the tiny end of the spectrum live coffee fines: dust-like micro-fragments that extract almost instantly and behave in ways the rest of your grind doesn’t. Getting a handle on them is one of the clearest paths from “my brew is okay” to “I finally understand why my brew tastes the way it does.”

What exactly is a coffee fine — and how does it form?

When you roast a coffee bean, it becomes brittle. When you grind it, the cellulose structure snaps in different places. A fine is essentially a cell-wall fragment with no intact cells, and that distinction matters because particles with intact cells extract very differently.

Fines are the smallest particles created when you grind coffee. Depending on the source, they might be defined as any particle under 100 microns in diameter, or as any particle smaller than the bulk of the ground coffee, or specifically as particles consisting only of cell fragments. For reference, 100 microns is one tenth of a millimetre — invisible to the naked eye and easy to inhale if you open a grinder too quickly.

Finely ground coffee for espresso has a characteristically bimodal particle-size distribution. For a given median grind size, different grinding technologies can yield very different shares of fines, defined as particles under 100 µm. That bimodal shape (two humps on a distribution curve rather than one clean peak) is why grind uniformity gets discussed so seriously in specialty coffee circles.

Why do fines matter for flavor?

A question we hear often: “I bought a decent grinder — shouldn’t fines stop being a problem?”

Fines extract almost instantaneously, which can mean harsh bitterness and astringency in the cup. Boulders, the opposite extreme, are oversized chunks that barely extract at all during a normal brew, contributing flat or watery flavors. Both extremes fight each other in the same brew, which is why an uneven grind can taste simultaneously bitter and weak.

Even premium burr grinders produce 15-25% fines and oversized particles that hurt extraction. These inconsistencies create what coffee scientists call “bimodal distribution” — two peaks of particle concentration that extract at different rates and push the cup in opposite directions.

The situation is more nuanced than “fines = bad,” though. Fines can either stick to larger particles or to other fines, and each scenario tastes different. When fines attach to bigger particles, they act as part of that larger particle during extraction and don’t over-extract the way they do when they’re only clinging to other fines. This is why some baristas who expected sifting to always improve their cup found mixed results: the fines bonded to coarser particles were actually contributing positively.

Do fines behave differently in espresso vs. filter brewing?

This one comes up a lot: “My barista friends say fines are actually good for espresso. Is that true?”

Yes. Fines play a different role depending on your brew method, and the research is pretty clear on this.

Fines are extremely important to espresso extraction. Espresso relies on erosion from the surface of particles, so the high surface area fines provide is genuinely useful there. In filter brewing, you have longer contact time and lower pressure — conditions that let loose fines over-extract aggressively before the rest of the bed has finished.

A 2024 study published in Scientific Reports by researchers at the Zurich University of Applied Sciences looked directly at fines’ role in espresso extraction. The researchers found no sensory penalty when using coffee with a higher share of fines. In fact, two samples with 1 and 2 grams of added fines were among the highest-scoring espresso shots in the experiment.

For filter brewing, the story flips. Fines are small enough to lodge in paper filter fibers, restricting flow and unpredictably increasing brew time. Removing them stabilizes drawdown, especially in pour-over methods like the V60 or Chemex. A clogged filter that stretches your V60 from 3 minutes to 5 minutes isn’t a technique problem — it’s often a fines problem.

What are boulders, and how do they fit in?

Readers frequently ask: “I keep hearing about fines, but what are ‘boulders’?”

Boulders are the large, underground chunks at the other extreme of the particle distribution. By one commonly cited definition, fines are particles under 300 microns and boulders are particles over 1,100 microns, though thresholds vary by source. Where fines extract in seconds, boulders may barely extract at all during a normal brew, contributing sour, underdeveloped, or watery flavors.

A useful way to picture your grind: imagine a bell curve. A well-calibrated grinder produces a narrow bell with most particles clustered tightly in the middle. An entry-level blade grinder (or worn burrs) produces a wide, flat curve with significant populations at both extremes. The tighter your distribution, the more predictable your extraction — and the more consistently you can repeat a great cup.

How does sifting actually work — and is it worth it at home?

Sifting gained traction largely through competitions. World Brewers Cup competitors, always after marginal gains, began experimenting with sieving their grounds to get more uniform particle distribution. The idea spread fast: if you control particle size, you control extraction.

In practice, sifting uses dedicated mesh sieves to separate ground coffee by particle size, targeting fines and boulders at each extreme. Tools like the Kruve sifter use two stacked sieves with different mesh sizes. The system takes your ground coffee and sifts it through both sieves at once, trapping oversized and undersized particles and leaving you with a specific, narrow range suitable for your brew method.

Is sifting worth it? That depends on your setup and your tolerance for extra steps:

French press brewing typically works best with coarser particles retained by 600-800 micron sieves, while espresso benefits from finer selections using 300-500 micron screens.

One practical note: ground coffee carries a lot of static charge created when particles are broken apart. The finest particles tend to stick to larger particles and aren’t readily removed by sieving — even with a rigorous protocol. Sifting isn’t a magic bullet; some fines will cling to the useful middle fraction no matter how long you shake.

Can sifting compensate for a cheaper grinder?

A question we hear often: “I can’t afford a $500 grinder right now. Can sifting close the gap?”

Partially — and honestly, yes, more than most people expect. Sifting produces brighter, fruitier coffee flavors and a uniform grind that means easily repeatable pour times. You can take a cheap grinder and get noticeably closer to coffee-shop results from it.

That said, the waste math matters. The wider a grinder’s distribution, the more material ends up in the fines and boulders chambers — typically discarded or repurposed (coffee fines make decent garden fertilizer or a mildly abrasive scrub). If 20% of your dose ends up in the discard chamber, you need to grind proportionally more to hit your target dose. Over time, that adds up in both cost and grinder wear. Using the Percolate app to track each bag — dose weight, brew notes and all — can help you see whether your sifted cups are consistently better, which makes the extra step feel earned rather than arbitrary.

A practical starting point for home sifters

If you want to experiment without buying dedicated equipment:

  1. Grind at your usual setting. Don’t change anything yet — you want a baseline.
  2. Shake gently through a fine kitchen strainer (around 300-500 microns if labelled). Not perfect, but instructive.
  3. Weigh what falls through — that is your fines fraction. Compare it to your total dose.
  4. Brew only the retained grounds (top of the strainer) and compare directly to your normal cup, brewed side by side.
  5. Adjust your recipe — removing fines reduces total extraction, so you may need to grind slightly finer or use a slightly higher dose to compensate.

Sifting removes fines and boulders, producing more consistent extractions. Uniform particle size gives you more control over flavor development, and sifting can improve clarity noticeably in competition-level coffees. The same logic applies at home, even with informal tools.

FAQs

What exactly are coffee fines? Fines are the smallest particles created when grinding coffee — generally defined as particles under 100-200 microns in diameter. They are essentially cell-wall fragments with no intact cells, meaning water can reach all their exposed surface at once, causing them to extract almost instantaneously.

Do coffee fines make my brew taste bitter? In filter brewing, loose fines that aren’t attached to larger particles can over-extract rapidly, contributing harsh bitterness and astringency. In espresso, research published in Scientific Reports (2024) found no sensory penalty from a higher share of fines — and some samples with added fines were among the highest-scoring shots in the study.

What is the difference between fines and boulders in a coffee grind? Fines are the sub-100-200 micron dust-like fragments at the smallest extreme. Boulders are the oversized chunks at the other end — typically particles above 1,000-1,100 microns — that remain stubbornly underextracted, producing flat, watery, or thin-tasting coffee. A good grind minimizes both.

Does even an expensive burr grinder produce fines? Yes. Research suggests 15-25% of any grind may consist of fines and oversized particles. No grinder produces a perfectly uniform output; the difference between budget and high-end grinders is how narrow the distribution is — not whether fines appear at all.

Should I sift my coffee grounds to remove fines at home? Sifting can improve clarity and reduce bitterness in pour-over or drip brewing, and it stabilizes drawdown time. It is time-consuming, causes some waste, and the benefit is less clear-cut for espresso. It’s a genuine marginal gain worth exploring if you want more out of an entry-level grinder.

What mesh size should I use to sift coffee fines? For filter brewing (V60, Chemex, drip), a 200-400 micron lower sieve removes the most problematic fines. For French press, a coarser 600-800 micron sieve works better for retaining useful body. Sifting systems like the Kruve use two stacked sieves simultaneously to remove both fines and boulders in one step.
