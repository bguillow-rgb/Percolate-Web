What Is Wet Hulled Coffee? Giling Basah Explained

By Percolate · Published September 8, 2026

Quick Answer

Wet hulled coffee (Giling Basah) is a processing method used almost exclusively in Indonesia, primarily Sumatra. The parchment layer is stripped from the bean while it is still at 35, 50% moisture, far earlier than any other process, producing a distinctively earthy, full-bodied, low-acid cup.

Wet hulled coffee is one of specialty coffee’s most misunderstood processing methods, and one of the most polarizing. If you’ve brewed a Sumatra and immediately noticed thick body, low acidity, and flavors somewhere between dark chocolate, cedar, and damp forest floor, you’ve already tasted it. Here’s what’s actually happening to that bean before it reaches your bag.

What does “wet hulled” actually mean, and why haven’t I heard of it?

A question we hear often: most specialty coffee education focuses on three processing methods: washed, natural, and honey. Wet hulling sits outside that mainstream conversation, partly because it is geographically narrow.

Wet hulling, or “giling basah” in Bahasa, is only practiced in Indonesia, particularly on Sumatra and Sulawesi.

Most other processing methods are more or less global. You can find washed, natural, and honey process coffees from nearly every coffee-producing region. That geographical isolation is exactly why home brewers often encounter the term once, find it confusing, and never get a clean explanation.

The name itself doesn’t help. “Wet hulled” is easily confused with “wet processed,” another name for washed coffee, which is the world’s most common processing method. But the two produce dramatically different results: wet hulling emphasizes body and mutes acidity, while wet processing highlights delicate acidity and sweetness. They sound similar. They taste nothing alike.

How does the wet hulling process actually work, step by step?

The process starts just like a washed coffee: cherries are picked, de-pulped, fermented, and washed. The departure happens in the drying phase. Where washed coffees go onto raised beds or patios for several days, coffees destined for wet hulling go straight to the hulling machine after washing, where the parchment is stripped off.

At that point, the coffee is typically at 35, 50% moisture, far higher than the 10, 12% at which most coffees are hulled. That’s the defining intervention: removing the protective parchment from a bean that is still essentially wet.

Worth noting here: in most producing countries it’s parchment coffee that gets spread to dry. In the wet hulled process, it’s what we call green coffee. Removing the parchment that early creates a distinct flavor profile, but it also adds risk, since the seeds themselves are now more vulnerable to environmental factors.

After hulling, the swollen, whitish-green beans go back out to finish drying, which now takes only a few more days down to 11, 14% moisture. Then it’s sorting, grading, bagging, and export.

Why did Indonesian farmers invent this method in the first place?

This one comes up a lot: it seems counterintuitive to remove the parchment so early. The reason is entirely practical.

The giling basah process started, generally speaking, at the end of the 1970s in Lintong, North Sumatra. It caught on among smallholders dealing with specific regional challenges: long distances to market, unreliable drying conditions with sudden fogs and sporadic rain, and a real need for quick cash.

It’s estimated that 99% of Indonesia’s coffee is grown by smallholders, many of whom farm coffee alongside other crops. Wet hulling lets them process and sell within days rather than weeks. That speed matters when cash flow is your primary concern.

Sumatra grows about 60% of all Indonesian coffee, and nearly all of it goes through some form of giling basah. According to the International Coffee Organization, Indonesia is the fifth-largest coffee producer in the world, so this method shapes an enormous slice of global supply.

What does wet hulled coffee taste like?

Readers frequently ask: “Is it always earthy? Is that a defect?”

The earthy, herbal, and savory notes that define wet hulled coffee are not defects. They are an inherent and intentional consequence of the process. Unlike washed or natural coffees, wet hulling removes the parchment at high moisture content, which produces a bold, full-bodied cup with earthy and herbal character built in.

Those specific flavor compounds develop because the unprotected seed is exposed to air, humidity, and microorganisms during the final drying phase. The beans take on a dark blue-ish color and a more intense flavor precisely because that protective coating is gone.

At its best, a carefully sourced, well-dried wet hulled Sumatra can offer dark chocolate, cedar, tobacco leaf, dried herb, and a syrupy, lingering mouthfeel that no washed African or Central American coffee quite replicates. The acidity is deliberately low. Body is the whole point. At its worst, poor execution produces musty, astringent, or overtly fermented cups, which is why sourcing quality matters so much with this process. Modern drying techniques, raised beds in particular, have reduced defects and made wet hulled coffee more consistent in specialty markets.

Which coffees on my shelf are wet hulled?

If a bag says Sumatra, Mandheling, Gayo, Lintong, or Sidikalang, it is almost certainly wet hulled. Mandheling, though often thought of as a growing region, is more of a trade name for Indonesian wet hulled coffee exported from Sumatra. The name came from the Mandailing ethnic group, who were historically coffee traders.

Indonesian farmers use the wet hull method for several origins: Sumatra coffees (Gayo, Sidikalang, Lampung), Sulawesi coffees (Toraja), and Java Arabica coffees from the Ijen plateau. If you’re unsure whether your bag went through giling basah, look for any of those names. Some specialty roasters now print “Giling Basah” explicitly on the label. If you see it, that’s your confirmation.

If you’re building a coffee shelf across multiple bags and origins, apps like Percolate let you log the process method alongside your tasting notes for each bag. That’s useful for tracking patterns like how wet hulled bags tend to behave over the degassing window compared to washed lots.

Is wet hulled coffee considered specialty grade?

This one comes up a lot, especially from brewers who associate earthiness with lower-quality commodity coffee.

Wet hulling does introduce more potential for defects, and it demands more careful handling at the mill and drying stage than washed processing. More processing stages mean more room for error. But high-quality wet hulled lots absolutely reach specialty-grade scores on the SCA’s 100-point cupping scale (80+ is the threshold for specialty designation; see our guide to what is a coffee cupping score).

The challenge is that bold earthy character can feel foreign to palates trained on bright washed Ethiopians or fruity Colombians. That’s a preference issue, not a quality issue. Wet hulled coffees are especially sensitive to harvest variation because the beans are exposed without protective layers during their most vulnerable drying phase, so the same farm can taste noticeably different from one year to the next.

How should I brew wet hulled coffee at home?

The flavor profile, heavy body, low acidity, savory-earthy notes, responds well to methods that play up texture rather than brightness:

  • French press is the classic pairing. Full immersion preserves the oils and weight that define a wet hulled cup. Use a medium-coarse grind and a 4-minute steep.
  • AeroPress works well with a longer steep (2, 3 minutes) and a medium grind. You can dial in the intensity easily.
  • Pour over can tame the earthiness slightly and bring out sweetness or chocolate notes. Use water around 93, 95°C and a medium grind. Rushing the pour amplifies bitterness.
  • Espresso from wet hulled Sumatras is less common in specialty shops but can produce an intensely syrupy, dark-chocolate shot. Dial for a longer pre-infusion time to even out extraction (see our guide to espresso pre-infusion).

Regardless of method, rest the beans for at least 7, 10 days post-roast before brewing. Wet hulled coffees often need a slightly longer degassing window than washed lots because of residual processing compounds. See our coffee degassing guide for the full rest-window breakdown.

FAQs

Is wet hulled the same as wet processed or washed coffee? No, and this is the most common source of confusion. “Wet processed” is another name for washed coffee, which highlights acidity and clarity. Wet hulled (Giling Basah) is a separate and very different method: it removes the parchment while the bean is still extremely moist, producing low acidity and heavy body, nearly the opposite flavor profile.

Which coffees on my shelf are likely wet hulled? Any Sumatra (Mandheling, Gayo, Lintong, Sidikalang), most Sulawesi (Toraja, Kalosi), and some Flores coffees from Indonesia are almost certainly wet hulled unless the bag explicitly says “washed” or “natural.” If the bag says “Giling Basah,” that confirms it.

Why does wet hulled coffee taste so earthy? Because the green bean is exposed to open air, without its protective parchment, while it is still very wet. This extended contact between the unprotected seed and the environment allows earthy, herbal, and savory compounds to develop. The friction and heat from the hulling machine also adds intensity to the profile.

Is wet hulled coffee lower quality than washed or natural? Not inherently. The method has more potential for defects if poorly executed, but high-quality wet hulled lots that are carefully dried on raised beds are fully capable of achieving specialty grade (80+ on the SCA scale). The flavor is simply radically different, not inferior.

What brew method works best for wet hulled coffee? Full-immersion methods like French press and AeroPress tend to complement wet hulled coffee’s naturally heavy body. Pour-over works too, especially if you want to tame the earthiness and highlight any sweetness. Avoid very fine grinds or long brew times, which can push astringency.
