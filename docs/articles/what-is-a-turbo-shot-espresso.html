What Is a Turbo Shot Espresso? Recipe, Science & Tips

By Percolate · Published August 23, 2026

Quick Answer

A turbo shot is an espresso extracted in roughly 15 seconds using a coarser grind and lower pressure (around 6 bar) than a traditional shot. The faster flow reduces channeling, raises extraction yield, and produces a sweeter, brighter cup, especially with light-roast, single-origin beans.

A turbo shot flips almost every rule of traditional espresso. Where the classic shot demands a fine grind, nine bars of pressure, and 25-35 seconds of extraction, the turbo shot does the opposite: coarser grind, lower pressure, done in roughly 15 seconds. And the cup is often sweeter and brighter than what the standard recipe produces. If you’ve been chasing tasting notes that keep hiding behind bitterness, this technique is worth understanding.

Wait — what exactly is a turbo shot espresso?

A turbo shot is an espresso extracted at a higher flow rate than traditional technique, typically in 6 to 15 seconds rather than the standard 25 to 35. To get there, the grind is set coarser, which lets water pass through the puck faster without needing high pressure to force it through. The result is a shot that is faster, often less bitter, and when done correctly, surprisingly well-extracted.

The technique was popularized by Lance Hedrick around 2021, building on extraction theories from Scott Rao and scientific work by Promethium/Hendon. That research explored how grind size, pressure, and puck saturation affect espresso extraction. Hedrick’s argument was that many traditional 9-bar extractions suffer from uneven puck wetting, which leads to channeling and inconsistent flavor.

Interestingly, the turbo shot was initially seen as a mistake in espresso preparation before it gained acceptance — a reminder of how much the definition of a “good” shot has shifted over time.

Where did the turbo shot come from — is this a gimmick or real science?

A question we hear often: is this just specialty-coffee Twitter hype, or does the extraction science actually hold up?

The turbo shot gained traction in espresso discussion groups and forums around early 2020, following publication of a study titled “Systematically Improving Espresso.” That research, led by coffee professionals and scientists including Dr. Christopher Hendon and Michael Cameron, looked at ways to optimize espresso extraction for consistency and flavor.

The core insight is about flow dynamics. Coarser grinds reduce flow resistance and channeling, letting water contact all coffee particles evenly and producing a higher extraction yield. In traditional espresso, ultra-fine grinds create resistance that the pump fights against at 9 bar, and that fight often ends with a puck that channels unevenly. At 9 bars, high-velocity water can compress a coarse puck, causing it to seal shut or channel violently. At 6 bars, the water moves gently through the coarse matrix.

Research shows that turbo shots achieve extraction yields between 19-22%, matching or exceeding traditional shots, even though the brew time is a fraction of the length. The math works because uniform saturation beats brute-force contact time when it comes to pulling soluble compounds out evenly.

What does a turbo shot actually taste like?

This one comes up a lot: people assume “faster” means “weaker” or “sour.” Done right, it’s the opposite.

Expect high clarity, intense sweetness, and reduced bitterness, with a lighter body (lower TDS) compared to traditional shots. Complex light roasts come through with particular clarity and sweetness. The coarser grind and reduced pressure mean less channeling, and the method avoids the over- and under-extracted zones that plague traditional shots.

The trade-off is body. A turbo shot is significantly sweeter and cleaner, but it has less texture than a traditional 9-bar espresso because of the lower Total Dissolved Solids. If you prize a thick, syrupy mouthfeel above everything else, it may feel underwhelming. But if you’re buying expensive single-origin light roasts and finding they taste muddled or bitter in the cup, a turbo shot might be the lens that finally lets you taste what’s actually on the bag label.

Turbo shots are well suited to lighter roasts, which are increasingly what specialty coffee drinkers are reaching for.

How do I actually pull a turbo shot — what’s the recipe?

Readers frequently ask: whether they need special equipment. You don’t, but a pressure gauge helps.

Here’s a practical starting-point recipe based on widely reported parameters:

ParameterTraditional EspressoTurbo Shot
Dose18-20 g15 g
GrindFineNoticeably coarser (between espresso and drip)
Pressure9 bar~6 bar
Extraction time25-35 seconds10-15 seconds
Yield~36 g (1:2 ratio)~40-45 g (1:2.6-1:3 ratio)
BodyFull, syrupyLighter, cleaner
BitternessModerate-highLow
SweetnessModerateHigh

The target recipe is 15 g in, 45 g out in 15 seconds at 5-6 bar. Grind noticeably coarser than your usual espresso setting. Start the extraction, monitor pressure via gauge, and aim for 5-6 bar. The shot should yield 45 g in approximately 15 seconds.

After pulling, check the puck: it should be evenly saturated. An even, wet puck is your visual confirmation that water moved through the grounds uniformly rather than blasting a channel through one side. The shot will look pale and flow fast. Resist the urge to grind finer out of habit. Taste it. A good turbo shot is sweet, round, and well-balanced.

Do I need a fancy pressure-profiling machine, or can I use what I own?

A question we hear often: this is where most home baristas get stuck, because true 6-bar pressure requires either machine modification or a pressure-profiling espresso machine.

The short answer: you can get meaningfully close on a standard single-boiler machine. While 6 bar is ideal, you can approximate a turbo shot on standard machines by dosing lower (15 g), grinding coarser, and aiming for a 1:3 ratio in 15-20 seconds. Modifying the OPV spring on a Gaggia to 6 bar gets you even closer.

You don’t need a pressure-profiling machine. Just monitor pressure via a gauge and control flow through grind size. On a fixed 9-bar machine, the coarser grind does most of the heavy lifting. The water still finds less resistance and flows faster, even if pressure stays at 9 bar. You’ll get a noticeably different shot — and often a better one — than your previous fine-grind attempts with the same light-roast beans. Think of it as roughly 80% of the benefit with none of the hardware cost.

What beans should I use for a turbo shot?

Light to medium-roasted single-origin Arabicas are the right starting point. The key is freshly roasted, fruit-forward beans that do well under quick, low-pressure brewing. Ethiopian washed coffees with floral, tea-like profiles, or Colombian naturals with clean berry sweetness, tend to reward the technique with remarkable clarity.

Almost everyone agrees the method does not suit medium-to-dark roasts well. The faster extraction can amplify bitterness, and dark roasts have already lost many of the delicate volatile compounds during roasting — the reduced contact time in a turbo shot doesn’t help them develop structure. If your shelf currently runs dark, stick with traditional parameters and explore lighter offerings before switching techniques.

Is a turbo shot the same thing as a bad fast shot?

Readers frequently ask: whether a pale, fast-flowing shot is just a gusher by another name.

No. A bad fast shot, or “gusher,” channels unevenly and tastes sour or astringent. A turbo shot uses a coarse grind to ensure even water flow across the whole puck, producing high extraction and a sweet cup despite the speed.

The diagnostic is simple: taste it. Sour and thin means a gusher, channeled through one spot and under-extracted. Sweet, bright, and round means a turbo shot, evenly extracted despite the speed. If you’re in the sour camp, coarsen your grind further. Counterintuitive, but that’s the adjustment that reduces channeling, not increases it.

The starkest difference between lungos and turbo shots is pull time: the lungo’s is roughly four times longer. A turbo shot gives you a brewing ratio of 1:2.6 and a final beverage weight of 40 grams, compared to the lungo’s 1:5.3 ratio and 80-gram yield. FAQs

What is a turbo shot espresso? A turbo shot is an espresso pulled with a coarser grind, around 6 bar of pressure, and a short extraction time of roughly 15 seconds. The result is a sweeter, brighter shot with high extraction yield and lower bitterness compared to a traditional 9-bar, 25-35 second pull.

Can I pull a turbo shot on a standard home espresso machine? Yes. On a fixed 9-bar machine, dose lower (around 15 g), grind noticeably coarser, and aim for a 1:3 ratio in 15-20 seconds. You won’t be at true 6 bar, but the coarser grind does most of the work. Machines with an adjustable OPV get closer to the ideal technique.

What beans work best for a turbo shot? Light to medium-roast single-origin Arabicas — especially fruity Ethiopian or Colombian naturals — shine in turbo shots. The fast extraction amplifies bright, sweet notes. Avoid dark roasts: the high flow rate can amplify bitterness in heavily roasted beans.

Is a turbo shot just an accidentally fast (bad) shot? No. A bad fast shot happens because of channeling — water bypasses most of the grounds, leaving an under-extracted, sour result. A turbo shot uses a coarser grind intentionally so water flows evenly through the whole puck, achieving a high, uniform extraction yield despite the speed.

How does a turbo shot differ from a lungo? Both can use lower pressure and coarser grinds, but a lungo pulls for 35-60 seconds at a 1:5+ ratio. A turbo shot hits around 1:2.6-1:3 in just 15 seconds. The turbo shot is more concentrated and sweeter; the lungo is longer and can pick up more bitter late-extraction notes.

Does a turbo shot have less caffeine than a regular espresso? Not significantly. A turbo shot carries roughly the same caffeine as a double shot, around 60-100 mg. The short extraction time is offset by the coarser grind’s even saturation and high extraction yield.
