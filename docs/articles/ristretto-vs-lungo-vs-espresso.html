Ristretto vs Lungo vs Espresso: What's the Difference?

By Percolate · Published August 8, 2026

Quick Answer

Ristretto, espresso, and lungo are the same dose of ground coffee pulled with different amounts of water. Ristretto (~1:1 ratio) is the most concentrated and sweetest. Standard espresso (~1:2) is balanced. Lungo (~1:3) extracts longer and tastes bigger but more bitter. The difference is ratio, not a different drink.

When you order at a specialty cafe and the menu lists ristretto, normale, and lungo as options, or when your espresso machine’s app asks which “style” you want, it can feel like three completely different drinks. They’re not. Ristretto, espresso, and lungo are the same ground coffee pulled three different ways on the same machine. The only variable is how much water you send through the puck. That one change, though, shifts flavor, texture, and caffeine in ways that are genuinely worth understanding.

Wait — are these actually three different coffees, or three versions of one?

A question we hear often: people assume each name refers to a distinct recipe, like ordering a flat white versus a cappuccino. Not quite.

In Italian coffee culture, ristretto, espresso, and lungo are three variations of the same extraction method. The coffee dose stays constant. Ristretto and lungo are simply different brew ratios, referring to the amount of liquid extracted versus the amount of ground coffee used. What we call plain “espresso” is the standard or normale ratio; ristretto is shorter, lungo is longer.

Think of them as a single dial. Turn it one way and you get ristretto. Turn it the other and you get lungo. Leave it centered and you have espresso. Once you internalize that, the menu suddenly makes a lot more sense.

What exactly is a ristretto, and why do specialty cafes love it?

The key difference in a ristretto is extraction time. It uses the same amount of ground coffee as a standard espresso but less water because extraction stops earlier, yielding about 15-20 ml in the cup. Denser, more concentrated, shorter. The ratio runs roughly 1:1 — for example, 18 g of ground coffee yields 18 g of liquid espresso.

Here’s the counterintuitive part: ristretto is not automatically more bitter than espresso. Because extraction stops early, fewer of the later-stage bitter compounds make it into the cup. The flavor skews toward sweetness, fruit, and bright acidity rather than roasty depth. Caffeine is actually slightly lower too, since caffeine compounds extract later in the pull. You’re trading volume for intensity.

This is why specialty roasters and competition baristas often default to ristretto-style ratios for very light, floral roasts. It showcases delicate origin character without pushing into extracted bitterness.

How is standard espresso defined, and what ratio am I actually ordering?

This one comes up a lot: even regular coffee drinkers are fuzzy on what “standard espresso” means in weight terms.

A standard espresso follows a 1:2 ratio of coffee grounds to liquid. Dose 18 g of coffee, aim for roughly 36 g in the cup. Extraction typically runs 25-30 seconds at around 9 bar of pressure. It’s the middle of the spectrum, picking up sweetness, acidity, body, and roast character at once.

In practice, the 1:2 rule is a starting point, not a law. Our deep-dive on espresso brew ratio covers how to adjust that number deliberately. But for understanding how ristretto and lungo relate to espresso, the 1:2 baseline is your reference point.

So what is a lungo, and how is it different from just watering down espresso?

Readers frequently ask: “Isn’t a lungo just weak espresso?” It’s a reasonable question, and the answer reveals something important about extraction chemistry.

A lungo (Italian for “long”) is made by extending extraction time and letting more water pass through the same dose of coffee. It typically yields 40-50 ml, with a ratio around 1:3 or higher — say, 18 g of coffee yielding 54 g of extracted espresso. Because the pull keeps going, it draws out more of the later-stage compounds, which means more bitterness. Grinding slightly coarser than you would for espresso helps keep that bitterness from turning harsh.

A lungo is not an Americano. An Americano is a standard espresso shot diluted with hot water after brewing. A lungo is brewed long through the puck from the start. The two processes produce genuinely different results: lungo has a heavier, extracted body, while an Americano tends toward cleaner, more tea-like texture.

Which one should I actually order — or pull at home?

A question we hear often: “I want to try all three, but which should I start with?”

The honest answer: it depends on the roast in your portafilter. Ristretto rewards lighter, fruitier, more delicate coffees — Ethiopian naturals, Kenyan washed lots, Gesha varieties. The early extraction highlights sweetness and florals while sidestepping extracted bitterness. If you have a lot of single-origins on your shelf, try ristretto on your brightest bags first.

Standard espresso is the safe starting point for any new bean. Once you’ve dialed in your baseline espresso brew ratio, you can nudge toward ristretto or lungo territory intentionally. Lungo earns its place with darker, more chocolatey roasts where the extra extraction pulls through complex bitter-sweet depth that a shorter pull would leave behind. If you tend to find darker espresso a bit flat or short on the palate, a lungo can extend the experience. Just remember to dial your grind slightly coarser — the most common lungo mistake is pulling it at an espresso grind and getting harsh, tannic results.

According to specialty coffee industry data, the vast majority of espresso served globally is pulled at or near a 1:2 ratio, making standard espresso the benchmark from which ristretto and lungo deviate deliberately, not accidentally.

Does roast level change which style works best?

Yes, significantly. Light roasts tend to be denser and more soluble, extracting quickly. With fresh light roasts, ristretto’s early stop preserves brightness and prevents the roasty bitterness that would appear in a longer pull. (Stale coffee will taste flat regardless of ratio — that’s a freshness problem, not a style problem.)

Dark roasts have already undergone more chemical transformation during roasting, and many of their nuances live in the later-stage extracted compounds. A lungo, or at minimum a full 1:2 espresso, tends to do them more justice than a ristretto, which can make a dark roast taste underdeveloped and overly thick. This also explains why traditional Italian espresso culture, which has long favored darker roasts, gravitates toward the normale and occasionally the lungo over the ristretto.

When you’re tracking bags in Percolate, noting which pull style you preferred for each coffee gives you an extra data point beyond roast level and origin. It’s one of the fastest ways to build a practical sense of how extraction style interacts with flavor.

5+ Frequently Asked Questions

Is a ristretto stronger than espresso? In concentration, yes. Ristretto uses half the water for the same dose, making it denser and more intense per gram. In total caffeine, it’s actually slightly lower because caffeine-bearing compounds extract later in the pull, and ristretto stops early.

Does a lungo have more caffeine than espresso? Slightly. The longer pull extracts more of those later-stage caffeine compounds, landing around 80-90 mg versus 70-80 mg for a standard shot. The difference is real but not dramatic.

What is the brew ratio for ristretto, espresso, and lungo? Ristretto is approximately 1:1 (18 g in, 18 g out). Standard espresso is roughly 1:2 (18 g in, 36 g out). Lungo is around 1:3 or more (18 g in, 54 g out). All three use the same coffee dose — only the water yield changes.

Should I grind differently for a lungo? Yes. Grind slightly coarser than you would for espresso. The longer extraction time means finer grounds will over-extract and turn harsh.

What is the difference between a lungo and an Americano? A lungo pulls more water through the puck during extraction — it is brewed long. An Americano is a standard espresso shot diluted with hot water after brewing. Lungo has heavier extracted body; Americano is cleaner and more tea-like.

Which style is best for milk drinks like lattes? Ristretto is the specialty-cafe standard for milk-based drinks. Its sweetness and concentration cut through steamed milk without introducing the bitter, astringent notes a lungo might bring — and it doesn’t get lost the way a weak pull would. Many specialty shops have quietly moved their flat whites and cortados to ristretto bases for exactly this reason.
