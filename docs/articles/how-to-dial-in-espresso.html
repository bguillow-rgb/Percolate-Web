How to Dial In Espresso: Grind, Dose & Yield Explained

By Percolate · Published August 1, 2026

Quick Answer

Dialing in espresso means adjusting grind size, dose, and yield until shots taste balanced and pull in roughly 25-30 seconds at a 1:2 ratio (e.g., 18g in, 36g out). Always change one variable at a time, start with grind, and let taste -- not the clock -- be the final judge.

Every home espresso machine comes with an implicit promise: great coffee, consistently. What the manual rarely explains is that the machine alone can’t deliver on it. The missing step is dialing in, a short, repeatable calibration process that closes the gap between a sour, muddy shot and one that actually tastes like it was pulled by someone who knows what they’re doing.

What does “dialing in” even mean?

The phrase “dial in” describes making espresso taste as good as possible, essentially extracting the right amount of soluble flavor from the coffee using the right amount of water. It sounds technical. In practice it comes down to adjusting three numbers until the shot tastes balanced.

Those three numbers are grind size, dose, and yield. Think of it as calibration. The beans change, the humidity changes, the grinder burrs wear down. None of those numbers stay fixed forever, which is why dialing in is an ongoing practice rather than a one-time setup.

What are the three variables I’m actually adjusting?

A question we hear often: people new to home espresso assume the machine is responsible for shot quality. It isn’t. Your three levers are dose, yield, and grind size.

Dose is the weight of ground coffee in your portafilter, measured in grams. Most double baskets take between 17g and 19g. Pick a dose that fits your basket and leave it there while you work on everything else.

Yield is how much liquid espresso ends up in your cup, also measured in grams. The ratio of dose to yield, say 18g in to 36g out, is your brew ratio. That ratio is your recipe.

Grind size is your primary extraction control. Too coarse and water flows too fast, producing a sour, weak result. Too fine and water flows too slowly, producing something bitter and harsh.

Shot time, how long the pump runs, is not a variable you set. It’s what you read after the shot. A 27-second shot that tastes bitter is still a bad shot. Taste, not the clock, makes the final call.

Where do I start? What’s the baseline recipe?

This one comes up a lot: jumping in without a baseline means every adjustment is a guess. You need a fixed starting point.

The classic 1:2 brew ratio works well here. Pick a dose that suits your portafilter basket, 18 grams is a reasonable starting point for most standard double baskets, and target 36 grams of liquid espresso in the cup.

From there, set your grind to a medium-fine position and pull a shot, aiming for 25-30 seconds. Now taste it. That first shot gives you information even if it tastes terrible. It tells you which direction to move.

Done well, you can land a great shot from a new bag in three to five attempts.

How do I actually read a shot and know what to change?

This is where most guides get vague. Here’s the taste-to-adjustment map, as simply as possible:

What the shot tastes likeLikely causeFix
Sour, sharp, thinUnder-extracted (too fast)Grind finer
Bitter, hollow, dryOver-extracted (too slow)Grind coarser
Watery, weak bodyYield too high or dose too lowReduce yield by 3-4g, or add 0.5g to dose
Too intense, syrupyYield too lowIncrease yield by 3-4g
Balanced, sweet, roundDialed in (check)Write it down

Change one thing at a time. If you adjust dose, grind, and tamping pressure all at once, you’ll have no idea what actually made a difference.

One step people often skip: purge a small amount of coffee from your grinder after any grind adjustment. Old grounds at the previous setting will contaminate your next shot and make readings unreliable.

Why does my dialed-in recipe stop working after a few days?

Readers frequently ask: “I nailed a great shot on Tuesday and by Saturday the same settings taste flat. What happened?”

Two things shift a recipe over the life of a bag. First, beans degas. They release CO2 locked in during roasting, and you’ll usually grind finer over the first week or two as they settle. Second, humidity and temperature change how coffee flows through the grinder. Even the same beans from the same roaster will taste different a week off-roast versus three weeks off-roast.

This is where a simple log pays dividends. Recording dose, yield, grind setting, shot time, and roast date for each bag means you’re never starting completely from zero. Does roast level change how I dial in?

Yes, and it matters more than most beginners expect. Light roasts are denser and harder for water to penetrate, so they typically need a finer grind and may run longer. It’s not uncommon to pull a delicious light roast that takes over 40 seconds to reach target yield, well outside the conventional 25-30 second window, and still taste excellent. Dark roasts are more porous and extract quickly. Going too fine will push them bitter fast.

Brew temperature also plays a role. Most beginner machines run around 93 degrees C (200 degrees F), but if yours allows temperature adjustment, that’s worth exploring once you’ve locked in dose, ratio, and grind.

The principle stays the same regardless of roast level: anchor the dose and yield first, use grind size to control extraction, and let taste be your guide.

Do I really need a scale, or can I eyeball it?

Not reliably. Without a scale you’re guessing, and guessing doesn’t repeat.

Measuring yield by volume is particularly unreliable. Crema is unpredictable. Two shots that look identical volumetrically can be 3-4g apart by weight. Weight is repeatable; volume isn’t.

An entry-level espresso scale with a timer costs roughly $20-$40. It’s the highest-return upgrade available to any home espresso setup, including setups running machines worth ten times that amount.

When should I record a recipe and stop adjusting?

Once a shot tastes balanced, sweet and round with mild acidity and a clean finish, write down the settings: bean, dose, yield, time, and grinder number. That record becomes your starting point for the next bag of the same coffee.

The next bag won’t need identical settings. Roast date and batch both shift things. But you’ll be close enough to land in two shots instead of five.

Weigh everything, record your recipes, and change one variable at a time. Your palate sharpens with repetition, and the patterns come faster than you’d expect. 5+ FAQs

How long does it take to dial in espresso?

Dialing in is not guesswork. It’s a short, repeatable process. Done well, you can land a great shot from a new bag in three to five attempts.

Do I need to dial in every time I open a new bag?

Yes. Every new bag means starting the process again. Even the same coffee from the same roaster will shift, so expect to adjust.

Should I adjust grind or dose first?

Grind first, always. It has the biggest effect on flow and extraction. Lock your dose, target your yield, and use grind as your primary lever until the shot tastes balanced.

My shot tastes sour. What do I change?

Grind finer to increase extraction. Sour shots are almost always under-extracted. Water moved through too quickly and didn’t pull enough sweetness from the coffee.

Can I dial in without a kitchen scale?

Not reliably. A scale is the most important piece of espresso equipment after the grinder. Weigh your dose in, weigh your yield out, and log what worked. Everything else is guesswork.

What’s the difference between shot time and yield, and which matters more?

Shot time is a useful secondary indicator. It helps you catch problems like channeling or uneven flow, but yield and taste should guide your final decision. Time tells you something is wrong; taste tells you what to fix.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to dial in espresso?

Most home baristas can find a balanced shot in 3-5 attempts once they have a solid baseline recipe. With practice, the process narrows to 2-3 shots per new bag.

Do I need to dial in every time I open a new bag of coffee?

Yes. Even the same coffee from the same roaster will behave differently bag to bag, and the roast date changes how the beans degas and extract. Always start fresh with each new bag.

Should I adjust grind size or dose first?

Grind size first, always. It has the biggest impact on extraction speed and flavor. Lock your dose, hit your target yield, then use grind to dial in shot time and taste.

My shot tastes sour -- what do I change?

A sour shot is almost always under-extracted. Grind finer to slow the water down, extract more soluble compounds, and bring sweetness and balance into the cup.

Can I dial in espresso without a kitchen scale?

Not reliably. Measuring dose and yield by weight (in grams) is the only way to repeat a successful recipe. A basic espresso scale costs around $20-$40 and is the single highest-return investment in your espresso setup.
