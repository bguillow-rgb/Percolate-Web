What Is a Coffee Flight? How to Run One at Home

By Percolate · Published August 21, 2026

Quick Answer

A coffee flight is a set of 3-6 small cups of different coffees served side by side for direct comparison. Unlike a formal cupping, flights are casual and brew-method-flexible. They're one of the fastest ways to train your palate, understand what you like, and distinguish between origins, roast levels, or processing methods.

A coffee flight puts two, three, or more coffees in front of you at the same time so you can taste them back to back, directly comparing what makes each one different. It borrows the format from wine and beer flights, strips away the formal rules of a lab cupping, and turns comparing beans into something you can do on a weekend morning with gear you already own. If you’ve ever finished a bag and struggled to remember exactly how it tasted, or bought a new origin on instinct only to feel unsure whether you actually prefer it, a flight solves that problem at the source.

”Wait — isn’t this just cupping? What’s actually different about a flight?”

A question we hear often: and the distinction matters more than it sounds.

A home cupping follows a specific SCA protocol: identical grind size, a fixed 8.25g-per-150ml ratio, water poured at 93 degrees C, a four-minute steep, a formal break, and evaluation from spoons. The structure exists to eliminate brewing variables so scores are comparable across labs and roasters worldwide. It’s genuinely useful, but it’s also a ritual that requires some setup and commitment.

A coffee flight has no fixed rules. You pick a brew method you’re comfortable with, pull small servings of two or more coffees, and taste them side by side. Flights can be espresso-based (different origins or processing methods), filter or pour-over comparisons, a single coffee prepared three different ways, or a roast-profile progression from light to dark. The goal isn’t a score. It’s a moment of direct comparison that your memory simply can’t manufacture when you drink one bag at a time over three weeks.

“Unless you work in a cafe or have an impressive home bar set up, most people rarely have the opportunity to taste and compare different coffees in one sitting” — and flights exist to provide exactly that opportunity. For specialty drinkers who rotate through multiple bags, that side-by-side moment is often the single biggest leap forward in building a genuine sense of personal taste.

”How many coffees should I include, and how much of each?”

Readers frequently ask: whether more coffees means more learning. It doesn’t, at least not past a point.

Three is the sweet spot for beginners. Four to six works well if you’re more experienced. Much more than six and the tasting starts to blur.

For serving size, most flights use 2 to 3 oz per cup, sometimes with printed tasting notes alongside. At that volume you get enough liquid to evaluate aroma, first sip, mid-palate, and finish without filling up before you’re done comparing.

At a specialty shop, flights typically run $10 to $25 depending on what’s included, with premium single-origins or rare micro-lots pushing the price higher. At home, the only cost is whatever you’d spend on a couple of bags, and you’re left with enough coffee to keep brewing for days after.

”What theme should I pick for my first flight?”

This is where it gets genuinely interesting, because the theme determines what you learn.

For a first flight, an origin comparison is the most immediately rewarding approach. Pick two or three single-origin bags from different countries, keep the roast level and brew method identical, and let the origin do the talking. The differences between, say, a washed Ethiopian and a natural Brazilian are dramatic enough that most people can describe them on the first sip, even without formal tasting vocabulary.

A single-variable flight removes most of the noise. When origin, process, and batch size stay the same and only roast development changes, the effect of that one variable becomes much easier to recognize. The same logic applies to any single-variable design: fix everything except the one thing you want to understand.

”What gear do I actually need to run a flight at home?”

This one comes up a lot: especially from people who don’t own espresso equipment.

You need less than you think. Three clean brewers or cups, one grinder, a scale, water, and a way to keep the samples straight. A single pour-over dripper works fine if you brew sequentially and use a thermos or preheated cups to hold the earlier samples at temperature.

You can absolutely run a flight without an espresso machine. Drip, pour-over, French press, and cold brew all work. The one rule that matters most: keep every brew variable identical across all cups — same dose, same grind setting, same water temperature, same brew time. Any difference you leave in the process becomes noise that obscures the signal you’re trying to hear.

One practical tip from professional tasters: label the coffees A, B, and C on the bottom of the vessels so you can taste without being led by words like “light” or “dark.” Knowing which is which before you taste is a fast path to confirmation bias. You want your first impression to be uncontaminated.

”When in the brew should I taste, and what should I notice?”

Taste each coffee more than once. Start hot, return when the cups are warm, and taste again as they cool. Some differences only become clear once heat is no longer the loudest sensation in the cup.

As you taste, work through the same four checkpoints in every cup:

  1. Aroma — Before you sip, smell. Aroma accounts for the majority of what we perceive as “flavor,” and differences between coffees are often most vivid at this stage.
  2. Acidity — Is there brightness? Does it feel citrus-like, malic (apple), or phosphoric (clean and round)? High-elevation origins tend to show more defined acidity.
  3. Body — How does it sit in your mouth? Heavier bodies feel coating and viscous; lighter bodies feel tea-like and clean.
  4. Finish — What lingers after you swallow? A long, sweet finish often signals quality; a short or harsh one suggests under-extraction or stale beans.

“Having side-by-side servings of different coffees is the best way to perceive and distinguish between the various tastes and aromas.” That’s the core insight behind flights: your palate is fundamentally a comparative instrument. It notices differences far more reliably than it recalls absolutes.

”How does a coffee flight help me figure out what I actually like?”

Readers frequently ask: whether flights are really about learning, or just a fun novelty.

They’re both, and the learning is faster than almost any other approach. According to specialty coffee industry research, 35% of specialty coffee drinkers identify flavor as part of what makes a cup feel “special,” yet most people drink one coffee at a time, making it nearly impossible to build a vocabulary for what they prefer. A flight collapses weeks of sequential brewing into a single morning session.

The practical payoff: once you know you consistently prefer washed coffees over naturals, or that you gravitate toward high-elevation Latin American origins over Indonesian earthiness, you stop buying randomly and start building a shelf with intention. That’s exactly the kind of pattern that Percolate’s taste profile builds over time. Every bag you log, every cupping note you save, feeds back into a picture of what your palate actually responds to, so your next purchase is more informed than your last.

Use the flight as a variable in your own tastings: pick two coffees from the same region but at different altitudes, keep everything else constant, and taste them side by side. You’ll start to recognize how one variable expresses itself. Over time, you’re not just enjoying coffee — you’re building a mental map of flavor that makes every future cup richer.

”Is there a right order to taste the coffees in a flight?”

There’s no universal rule, but a few conventions make the experience cleaner.

Most tasters move lighter to darker within a roast-level flight. Lighter roasts are more delicate, and tasting them after a dark roast means you’ll miss nuance. For origin flights without a clear roast gradient, start with the coffee you expect to be most delicate and finish with the boldest. Between each cup, a few sips of still (not sparkling) water helps reset the palate without overcleaning it.

“While tasting coffee after it’s cooled lets you experience different flavours, everyone should experience flights in their own way — no order and no rules,” as one specialty coffee professional puts it. The point is curiosity, not ceremony. If you want to skip back to cup one after cup three surprises you, do it.

Frequently asked questions

How many coffees should be in a flight? Three is the ideal starting point for most home tasters — manageable without overwhelming your palate. Four to six works well once you have some experience. Much beyond six and the cups start to blur together.

Do I need an espresso machine to run a coffee flight at home? No. Pour-over, French press, AeroPress, and drip all work well for filter flights. For a concentrated espresso-style flight without a machine, a moka pot or AeroPress with a fine grind is a practical substitute.

What’s the difference between a coffee flight and a cupping? A cupping follows a strict SCA protocol — specific grind, water temperature, steeping time, and evaluation from spoons. A flight is informal: any brew method goes, cups are sipped, and the format is designed for enjoyment and exploration rather than scoring.

Should I taste coffee in a flight hot or after it cools? Both. Start hot to catch aroma and initial flavor, then revisit each cup warm and again as they cool. Many differences between coffees — especially acidity and sweetness — become much clearer as the temperature drops.

What’s the best theme for a first home coffee flight? Origin comparison is the most beginner-friendly theme: pick two or three single-origin beans from different countries, keep the roast level and brew method identical, and taste them side by side. The differences tend to be dramatic and easy to describe without any prior tasting experience.

How do I keep track of what I noticed in each cup? Write it down immediately — even one or two words per cup. Flavor memory fades fast, especially once you’ve tasted several things in a row. Percolate’s structured cupping notes format makes this easy to do on your phone in real time, so your observations from a flight become part of your long-term taste profile rather than disappearing the moment you rinse the cups.
