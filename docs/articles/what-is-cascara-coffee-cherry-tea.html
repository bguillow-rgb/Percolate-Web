What Is Cascara? The Coffee Cherry Tea Guide

By Percolate · Published November 10, 2026

Quick Answer

Cascara is a tea-like beverage brewed from the dried skin and pulp of the coffee cherry, the fruit that surrounds the coffee bean. It tastes fruity and floral, not like roasted coffee, and contains roughly one-quarter the caffeine of a standard cup of drip coffee.

Cascara sits in a strange, wonderful gap in the coffee world: it comes from the same plant as your morning espresso, yet it tastes nothing like it. If you’ve ever seen a bag of dried, reddish-brown husks at a specialty roaster and wondered what they were, this is your guide.

So what actually is cascara — is it coffee or tea?

A question we hear often: Is cascara a kind of coffee, or a kind of tea?

The honest answer is neither, exactly. Cascara is an infusion made from the dried outer skin and pulp of the coffee cherry — the fruit that surrounds the coffee bean. It is not a true tea (no Camellia sinensis involved), but a fruit tisane brewed from what most coffee producers once discarded.

The word cascara is Spanish for husk or peel, which describes exactly what you are steeping.

Cascara is the dried skin and pulp of the coffee cherry — the part discarded during normal coffee processing. After coffee cherries are pulped, the skin and remaining pulp are usually treated as agricultural waste: composted, used as fertilizer, or simply thrown out. Turning that by-product into a beverage is a relatively recent development in the specialty world, though the tradition itself is old. Yemeni qishr and Ethiopian cherry infusions have used coffee cherry husks for centuries.

Salvadoran coffee farmer Aida Batlle is credited with developing a secondary market for coffee cherry skins in the specialty era. Around 2005 she noticed the husks left over from milling had a floral aroma, and she decided to try brewing tea from them.

What does cascara actually taste like?

Cascara sits somewhere between coffee and tea — and despite coming from the coffee plant, it tastes like neither.

It delivers a naturally fruity profile with notes that can include dried cherry, hibiscus, honey, or subtle florals. The flavor varies depending on origin and processing, but the cup is generally bright, smooth, and gently sweet, with no roasted bitterness.

More specifically, the flavor tends toward red fruit character: cranberry, dried cherry, raisin, sometimes rosehip. The body is lighter than coffee, closer to fruit tea.

One important nuance: there are two distinct types of cascara depending on how the coffee was processed. Pulp-based cascara comes from washed or honey-processed coffee, while husk-based cascara comes from natural-processed coffee, where the cherries dried with the beans still inside. Pulp-based cascara tends to be more tangy, while husk-based cascara is deeper and sweeter. Just like with roasted coffee, terroir matters: where the fruit was grown and how it was dried both leave a clear mark on the cup.

How much caffeine does cascara have?

This one comes up a lot: People assume that because cascara comes from the coffee plant, it must be a serious caffeine source — or, at the other extreme, that it’s caffeine-free.

Neither is true. Square Mile Coffee Roasters sent cascara to a lab in Germany to test its caffeine content. Even at the strongest, longest brew, cascara came in at 111.4 mg/L, compared to roughly 400-800 mg/L in brewed coffee.

In a standard 250 ml cup that works out to somewhere around 25 to 30 mg of caffeine — about what you’d get from a green tea, and comfortably less than a black tea. Anyone monitoring caffeine intake, whether during pregnancy, with sensitivity, or for sleep reasons, should count cascara as part of their daily total. It’s not nothing, but it’s genuinely mild compared to even a half-strength cup of coffee.

How do I brew cascara at home?

Cascara is forgiving, and there’s room to experiment. Here’s a practical framework:

VariableHot BrewCold Brew
Ratio15-20 g per 1 L water15-20 g per 1 L cold water
Temperature190-200 degrees F (88-93 degrees C)Refrigerator-cold
Steep time4-6 minutes12-24 hours
ResultBright, slightly tart, aromaticCleaner, sweeter, lower acidity
EquipmentFrench press, tea strainerFrench press, mason jar

A baseline of 15-20 grams per liter, 190-200 degrees F, steeped for 4-6 minutes produces a balanced hot cup. You can adjust steep time to taste — longer steeps deepen the fruit character — but steep time barely moves the caffeine while the ratio moves it a lot. A long steep gives you more flavor without much more stimulant.

Some cascara is made specifically for cold brewing. A 12-24 hour cold steep produces a cleaner, sweeter beverage. Skip the milk: the acidity in the fruit will curdle it, and the flavor has nothing to gain from dairy anyway. A drizzle of honey or a cinnamon stick, on the other hand, works beautifully.

Is cascara sustainable? Where does it come from?

Readers frequently ask: Is buying cascara actually good for the environment and the farmer?

This is one of the genuinely encouraging stories in the coffee supply chain. Cascara uses the roughly 50% of the coffee cherry that would otherwise be waste. Dry processing yields roughly 1 kg of husk per kg of coffee bean, making cascara a direct farm by-product worth buying.

Coffee cherry is also sold as ready-to-drink beverages, syrups, and concentrated extracts, reflecting the growing commercial use of coffee fruit by-products across food and beverage. When you buy cascara from a specialty roaster who sources it traceably, you’re giving that producer a second revenue stream from the same harvest. Named estate, named variety — good cascara is traceable for the same reason good coffee is: someone is proud of it.

The median bag of specialty coffee costs $20 in 2026, and well-sourced cascara typically falls below that price point, making it an accessible way to explore a different dimension of the same plant.

How is cascara different from regular coffee — a quick comparison?

The two beverages share a plant but almost nothing else. If you enjoy hibiscus tea, dried fruit, or a naturally sweet tisane, cascara is likely to feel familiar from your very first cup.

Where does cascara fit in a home coffee practice?

If you’re already logging bags and tracking tasting notes — say, using an app like Percolate to build your coffee shelf — cascara is a natural curiosity to add alongside your roasted beans. It won’t replace your V60 morning ritual, but it offers a completely different sensory angle on the same agricultural product you already love. Brewing it right before or after a roasted coffee from the same origin can be a genuinely illuminating comparison.

Cascara tastes a lot more like the raw coffee cherry than roasted coffee does, because the bean undergoes a long transformation before it reaches your cup, while cascara is much closer to coffee as it exists in nature. That’s worth experiencing at least once.

5 FAQs about cascara

Is cascara the same as coffee? No. Coffee is brewed from the roasted seed inside the coffee cherry. Cascara is brewed from the dried outer skin and pulp of that same fruit — it has no roasted character and tastes nothing like coffee.

How much caffeine is in cascara? Laboratory analysis measured about 111 mg of caffeine per liter at full strength, compared with 400 to 800 mg per liter for brewed coffee. That puts cascara at roughly a quarter of a cup of coffee, in the same range as a green tea.

What does cascara taste like? Cascara has a fruity, mildly sweet, tangy character often compared to hibiscus tea, dried cranberry, tamarind, or dried cherry. Well-made cascara has a distinctive berry and tropical-fruit quality that doesn’t taste like coffee at all.

Is cascara the same as cascara sagrada? No. In coffee, cascara refers to material from the coffee fruit, not the herbal laxative known as cascara sagrada. They share a Spanish root word but are entirely unrelated plants.

Can I brew cascara cold? Yes. Cold-brew cascara tends to be smoother and less acidic than hot-steeped versions. Use 15-20 g per liter of cold water, steep in the fridge for 12-24 hours, then strain and serve over ice.
